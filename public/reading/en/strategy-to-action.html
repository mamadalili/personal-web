From Strategy to Results

McKinsey frameworks, execution and learning

Three projects are on your desk and the team has limited time. How can a strategy framework help you choose? Who does what next, and what evidence changes the next decision? Follow a hypothetical coffee-equipment shop.

Three projects, one team

Imagine a coffee-equipment shop. Buyers ask which part fits their machine. You also want to develop regular coffee deliveries to offices and test a machine-servicing reminder. The team has a total of 12 hours available for these projects this week.

At the meeting you say, “We need to grow.” To help a colleague choose tomorrow morning’s work, that intention needs a clear choice, a concrete task and a review point. Strategy frameworks help you examine the question. An operating routine carries the choice into daily work.

The shop is hypothetical. Each numerical exercise states its units and method so you can check the result.

For the example shop, keep three questions in view. How well does the work support the shop’s promise? How much time reaches businesses at different stages of maturity? What returns to the decision meeting after the work is done?

Check alignment in the actual work

The 7S framework connects strategy, structure, systems, skills, staff, management style and shared values. To use it, follow a concrete situation across those elements. McKinsey’s 7S description

Our shop promises to help buyers find a compatible part. Sales needs to ask for the machine model, the product page needs the relevant information, and support needs to pass unclear questions to the content owner. Training and sales evaluation should support that promise too. If the reward only follows the number of sales, examine that connection at the meeting.

When the team says it needs better alignment, bring one customer message to the table and follow how that message gets answered.

Where does this week’s time go?

In the three horizons framework, the core business supports today’s income. An emerging business has customers and signs of growth. An early option still needs investigation and experiments. All three need attention concurrently, with review criteria suited to their maturity. Three horizons source

Here, the parts guide supports the current business. The office coffee service, with a hypothetical first paid order, is emerging growth. The servicing reminder is still an early option. The chart compares two allocations of the same 12 hours. Select an allocation name and inspect a horizon’s value.

The 8, 3 and 1 hour split is an educational example. To allocate your own team’s time, consider delivery commitments, customer needs and the evidence available for each project. At the end of the week, compare the planned time with the time actually spent.

Give each choice a working brief

The second post connects alignment, execution discipline and learning. For the shop, put those connections into a short working brief. The choice leads to an accountable owner and a concrete task. The task should produce evidence that helps the review. Mohart’s second post

Changing the project changes what belongs in its review. For the parts guide, inspect unresolved messages and return reasons. For the emerging service, inspect repeat buying and delivery costs. For an early option, look for a specific need and evidence that informs the next test.

Use the work report to make a decision

“Six conversations completed” helps you track the work. The next decision about the coffee service also needs notes on customer needs, repeat buying and delivery costs. If that evidence is still missing, the review can name the open question and assign an owner to find the answer.

Sales has held six conversations about the coffee delivery service. Which report would best inform a decision to expand it?

Only the conversation count and team hours These describe the work done. They leave the customer’s need, repeat purchases and the economics of delivery unresolved.

Customer needs, repeat orders and delivery costs alongside the work done This connects the work to evidence. It gives you reasons to continue or revise the service. A small trial still calls for care when judging a wider rollout.

A higher score for the idea in a strategy grid A score can help prioritize the discussion. After the trial, the next decision also needs evidence of customer behaviour and service costs.

Learning enters the routine when it changes the next piece of work. You might narrow the trial audience, revise the guide or set aside more time to investigate deliveries. Record the reason alongside the task so the next meeting can start from it.

What will you bring to the next meeting?

Choose one actual task and complete its brief with a colleague. Which choice does it advance? Who owns it? What evidence will you review, and when? Turn any unanswered question into a task someone can follow up.

At the next meeting, read the brief alongside what happened. Decide whether to continue, change part of the approach or stop the trial. The decision tool, daily work and learning then belong to one discussion you can follow through.
