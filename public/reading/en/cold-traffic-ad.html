An Ad for Someone Who’s Never Heard of You

From a jammed drawer to a reason to buy

Take the brand name out of the opening shot. Is anything left worth watching? Work through a fictional drawer-divider ad, frame by frame, and decide what each line needs to earn its place.

  • Find an observable behaviour for the opening shot.
  • Choose evidence for each product claim.

When the drawer sticks halfway

You’re cooking with one hand and pulling open the utensil drawer with the other. It stops halfway. The tongs have turned sideways again. Imagine putting “premium kitchen organizer” across that shot. Now remove the caption and mute the sound. You can still tell what happened, and you might stay to see how the cook gets the drawer open. That’s an opening worth filming.

Carneiro starts with product facts, then looks for people who share a specific frustration. “People who like cooking” gives the writer very little to film. Someone who regularly reaches through a narrow gap to free a utensil can show you a scene. Ask a buyer to reenact the last time it happened before you write the script. You might discover the ladle handle was the real culprit.

Four frames on the editing desk

Carneiro gives the ad four jobs. Earn a moment of attention, show why a solution could work, help the viewer judge its value, and give them a reason to act. Below, all four shots use the same drawer. Pick one to see the buyer’s question, a line of copy and the evidence you would have to bring back from the shoot.

Which line earns a shot?

“Your kitchen will stay tidy” is a tall order for one drawer opening. Shorten the claim until it fits the shot. Put the tongs back, close the drawer and open it again. If the divider slips along with the tongs, keep that failed take for the team. It tells you which line to cut or which installation step the instructions skipped.

One possible cut reads like this. “Stuck again? The tongs have swung across the drawer. Watch how much room they have to turn once I put a divider alongside them. Before you order, compare the inside depth of your drawer with this model’s guide.” Keep the price and buying terms wherever the viewer will make that decision.

If there’s time for one more take

Viewers finish the ad and message “Will it fit my drawer?” Which shot would you add to the next cut?

A closer shot of the jammed tongs That sharpens the opening. The viewer already recognizes the jam and still needs to know where to put the tape.

Both ends of the tape and the range for that model The viewer can now compare their measurement with the product range. Check the second fit condition too: the divider must clear the top of the drawer.

A wider shot of the tidy kitchen This gives the product a setting. Two drawers that look identical from across the kitchen can have different internal depths.

Make two cuts. In the second, replace only the product beauty shot with the measurement shot. Keep the audience, length, offer, dates and landing page the same. Randomly split the audience if the ad platform allows it. Decide in advance when you’ll count orders per product-page visit. Size-guide opens and sizing questions can help explain the result. Follow size-related returns through the return window, too.

If guide opens go up but there are too few orders or returns to compare, you’ve learned one thing: more visitors opened the guide. Listening to the questions those visitors still ask may be a better next edit than buying more impressions.
