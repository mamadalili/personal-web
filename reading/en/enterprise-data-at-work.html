Put Enterprise Data to Work

The warehouse says an item is in stock. Sales still can’t promise delivery. Follow one order to see which number the customer’s question actually needs.

IN THIS EXPERIENCE

  • Start with the customer’s question. Delivery tomorrow needs both an available item and room on the schedule.
  • Show whether the stock count includes reserved units and when someone last updated it.

Choose the decision first

“If I order today, can I have it tomorrow?” The salesperson opens the stock screen. There’s an item on the shelf, but it might already be reserved for another order. Tomorrow’s van might also be full. Before an AI tool can help answer the customer, the team needs to agree what separates “in the warehouse” from “available for delivery tomorrow.”

In his McKinsey interview, Antonio Castro suggests working backward from the decision. Here the salesperson needs to know whether she can name a date. The reservation record and delivery schedule matter more to her than a folder full of last month’s sales reports. It’s an approach the team can try on a single order.

Trace the answer

What is missing before accepting an order?

Open a card to see what the salesperson still needs to establish. The order is a teaching example.

Document responsibility too

The NIST risk framework calls for clear responsibilities and human oversight. In this order, the warehouse can correct a bad count, dispatch can flag a changed van schedule, and someone in sales needs to own the reply. Put each person’s name beside the job they can actually do.

What if the answer is correct but work stalls?

Sit with the salesperson after she finds the answer. She may have to copy the status from one screen into another. If she leaves the update time behind, the customer might receive a stale promise. Showing the time on the order screen could be a worthwhile first change.

Reporting data is not automatically working data

A month-end report is built to explain what happened last month. Its entry saying the item was in stock could be perfectly accurate. Sales has a different question. Which unit can be allocated to this particular buyer right now? Before reusing the report, check whether the timing and meaning of its number fit the job.

Trace one item you have permission to inspect with the warehouse staff. When does it become reserved, at the first request or after payment? If the buyer cancels, when does it go back on sale? Those answers tell the tool builder which entry to read and why it matters.

Must we clean every dataset first?

For a first trial, prepare just the records that this order uses. Once warehouse, sales and dispatch agree on definitions and timing, try them with the person on the phone. That tells the team what the next set of records will need before anyone sets about cleaning every file in the company.

An answer needs a traceable path

If the tool says “delivery tomorrow,” the salesperson should be able to trace that answer to its records. Perhaps the warehouse has confirmed free stock, but dispatch hasn’t confirmed space for that address. Seeing the two side by side lets her ask dispatch and explain to the customer exactly what is still open.

Following one answer can reveal where a word changed meaning between teams. The warehouse might record “shipped” when it labels a parcel, while sales reads it as “the van has left.” Record the two events separately for one order and check which one should appear in the customer’s message.

The customer doesn’t need a list of company systems. The salesperson needs to know which record is current, what it means and who can answer a question if it looks wrong.

The operating model is part of the product

An operating model describes who keeps things working after launch. Shifts change, orders get cancelled and former staff may no longer need access. Follow a delivery correction all the way through. When the van turns out to be full, who updates its capacity and who tells the customer the revised date?

Review a few permitted orders with sales. Did the confirmed date match the one the customer heard? Where did someone have to call back, and how long did it take to find the reason? Those answers tell you more about the tool’s value than a count of connected files.

Try one decision

Next time the question about tomorrow comes up, reopen a completed order for comparison. Could sales have made a sound promise with the information available that day? If the warehouse had the answer but sales couldn’t see it, you know which screen needs attention. If nobody records dispatch capacity, you first need someone to take that job.
