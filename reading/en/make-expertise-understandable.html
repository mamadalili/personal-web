Make Your Expertise Understandable

“Show the delivery charge earlier,” you tell a colleague. You know why, but they may have no idea where the customer got stuck. How do you turn that quick piece of advice into something someone else can understand and use?

IN THIS EXPERIENCE

  • Experience becomes useful to a reader when the problem, audience and practical consequence are clear.
  • A draft need not be perfect; unsupported claims, confidential details and high-stakes topics should not be rushed into public view.
  • Use a different shape for an opinion, a lived story or a recurring question, then sharpen the same idea with new evidence.

A useful idea may feel too obvious to mention

Logan Gott writes that when he interviews founders, someone will mention a detail in passing that’s new to him. The founder is surprised it needs explaining. After years on the job, you might spot a problem instantly. To someone sitting beside you for the first time, the moment you spotted it can be the most interesting part.

Picture a shop where you tell a colleague, “The buying process is broken.” You saw a customer looking for the delivery charge before paying. Your colleague didn’t. They might think the payment button failed. Starting with what the customer did gives both of you the same event to discuss.

Think of the last time a new colleague asked, “How did you know?” What were you looking at? Start there and you’ve got a first line.

Knowing and explaining are different skills

Sometimes you know a page has a problem before you can say how you spotted it. That’s one kind of tacit knowledge. In the shop example, the clues are that the buyer asks “What will I pay altogether?” and goes back to the basket. Put those details into words and your colleague can check where the charge appears and whether others had the same question.

Paul Graham argues that putting a thought into words reveals what you haven’t quite worked out. Try writing “The price wasn’t clear.” Do you mean the price of the item or the total including delivery? Pausing over that one sentence can separate two problems. Graham’s essay on putting ideas into words.

Try three sentences for the shop example. “The buyer asked about delivery before paying. I think they didn’t see the answer where they expected it. I don’t know whether that stopped them from buying.” Now a reader can tell what you saw and which part is your interpretation.

A thought need not be complete before you write

Give that short note to someone who hasn’t seen the page. They might ask, “Was delivery too expensive, or couldn’t the buyer find the price?” Their question exposes two problems the draft has blurred together. Even if it stays between the two of you, you now know which part needs work.

Gott says readers’ responses helped him sharpen his ideas. For a work example, first check what you have permission to share. If the piece concerns money, health, legal rights or customer data, check the evidence and permission before sending it out. You can raise the question now and save a firm conclusion for when you have enough information.

When should a draft stay private?

When you cannot connect the claim to a source or explainable experience, it contains someone else’s confidential information, or an error could seriously harm someone. Keep it private, remove identifying detail, seek qualified review or narrow the claim.

Answer two common fears with better questions

If you worry you might be wrong, start with the size of the claim. “This buyer asked about delivery before paying” can be checked against your note. “Everyone leaves because of delivery charges” would need different evidence. Even if the price was already on the page, you can still describe where this buyer looked for it.

You may think everyone has already written about delivery charges. The general advice might be familiar, but your colleague doesn’t know which part of this page a customer opened to look for the charge. Explain that observation. If another writer gave you the idea, credit them and show which part you observed or checked yourself.

Before publishing, ask what someone who already knows the general advice can take from your piece. One example that shows them where to look may be enough to write about.

The reader starts at line one, not inside your memory

Gott likens this to starting a book on page fifty. You write “We fixed conversion,” and your reader still doesn’t know whether you mean buying something or filling in a form. A reader entry point is the bit of context that makes the rest intelligible. “A buyer was looking for the delivery charge before paying” gives someone a reason to follow your review of the page.

Open the product-page example. Which detail helps a reader picture the situation without already knowing the shop? Try the same question on one of the other two topics. All three examples are made up for practice.

From raw note to usable message

Open an example, then identify the same three parts in your own experience.

If writing stalls, Gott suggests talking through the event for a few minutes and recording it for yourself. Listen back. What was your point? Who were you explaining it to? What would they understand better afterwards? If you can’t answer the last question, listen for the scene or decision you left out.

Give each kind of thought a fitting shape

You could start with the buyer’s question about delivery and answer it. If you recorded an actual event, you could tell the story of what happened. Or you could take a position on where to show the charge and explain your reasoning. Those are the three shapes Gott suggests. The tabs keep the delivery question fixed so you can see what each version asks of the writer.

Opinion

Position, evidence, implication

Position: “Delivery cost should become clear before the final buying step.” Evidence in a real article would be the store’s actual journey and recorded customer questions. Implication: show a range or calculation method at the right point instead of hiding the cost until the end. Without evidence, frame it as a testable proposal rather than a proven result. This adapts Gott’s Take–Proof–Edge shape.

Story

Before, action, result

Before: “A buyer had to reach the final step to learn the delivery cost.” Action: “We surfaced the calculation method earlier.” Result: state only what was actually recorded, such as whether recurring questions changed. If you have no outcome yet, stop the story at the action. This adapts Gott’s Before–Action–Result shape.

Question

Question, answer, example

Customer question: “When will I know my delivery cost?” Direct answer: “After choosing your city and delivery method, before payment.” Example: walk through a sample order to that point and state any exception there. Use this for questions people really ask, not staged FAQs. This adapts Gott’s Question–Answer–Example shape.

Each form asks for different material. A recommendation to move the price needs a reason. A customer story needs an event you actually observed. For an answer, read the buyer’s question again. Don’t explain why delivery is expensive when they asked where to find its price. The business website article applies the same approach to the page a customer lands on.

Make the opening clear, then support the claim

Gott suggests giving readers a reason to continue in the opening line. Compare “Today we’ll discuss website performance” with “Before paying, the buyer went back to the basket to find the delivery charge.” With the second opening you know what you’d like the next paragraph to explain. Where was the charge shown on the page?

The exercise offers two ways to open a piece about website enquiries. Which lets a reader picture who got stuck and where? Once you’ve chosen, ask a separate question. What evidence would you need before publishing that claim?

Which opening gives a new reader a clearer problem?

A reader might say, “But I could see delivery at the top.” Take that reply seriously. The page may have changed, they may have used a different phone, or you may have missed it. Check what you can and revise the sentence if needed. Saying which page you observed and when can help readers from the outset.

Repeat the idea, not the same paragraph

You don’t have to retire the subject of delivery after one post. Gott suggests that people come to understand a writer’s view through different examples. Next time a buyer finds the price but asks about the delivery date, you’ve got a new question worth writing about.

You can do this with speed, too. One piece shows an AI draft that was ready quickly but still needed fact-checking. Another begins with a customer whose enquiry went unanswered for a while. Both involve time, but the second needs the times that enquiry arrived and was answered. Evidence for the first story won’t fill that gap.

If a post gets little response, begin with what you know. Did the right reader find it? Start reading? Ask a question afterwards? Few replies alone won’t tell you where it broke down. The AI search measurement article explains why those questions need different measures.

AI can review the wording; it cannot supply your lived evidence

Gott suggests writing down your own observation first. Ask AI only for “an expert post about shops” and it won’t know where your buyer looked for the delivery charge. It may fill that gap with an invented story that reads perfectly smoothly.

Once you have your note, ask AI which sentence would confuse someone who hasn’t seen the page, and where the conclusion goes beyond the observation. Check its answers against what happened and keep private customer data out of the tool without permission. Whoever publishes the final piece should be able to stand behind its claims.

What is a useful AI review prompt?

“Here is my draft. Identify unclear sentences, unsupported claims and places where a new reader lacks context. Do not add facts, statistics, quotations or experiences. For each issue, tell me what question you need me to answer.” This keeps the tool in a review role and returns the factual answer to the author.

Make one real piece this week

Gott suggests spending 30–60 minutes gathering notes from experience, about ten minutes a day clarifying one idea, and publishing daily for thirty days to see how people respond. That’s his challenge. If your pieces need a customer’s permission or a colleague’s review, one per week may give you the time you need.

Start with a real question you’ve been asked more than once. Write who asked, what your answer left unclear and how you noticed. Then choose one of the three forms above. If you don’t know why the buyer left without ordering, leave that question open. What you do know may already be enough for a useful piece.

After publishing, ask a reader what they would check if they ran the shop. If they say “lower the delivery price” when you meant “make the price easier to find,” you know which sentence to revise. Views or silence alone couldn’t tell you that. Read your draft against the four checks below with that reader in mind.
