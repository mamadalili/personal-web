At Boomerang

What did it take to plan Boomerang’s online store?

I was responsible for launching and growing Boomerang’s online sales channels. I say I started a step before zero. The products were already selling, but the digital material amounted to a few incomplete renders. My first job was to gather the details for each model and put together photos and descriptions someone could use to place an order.

Picture two models that look identical from the front. One includes an extra part. Give both the same photo and bury the difference in a line of copy, and the buyer has to call to find out what’s in the box. That’s a question the store ought to answer right where the order happens.

Try buying it as a customer would

When you know the range by heart, you fill in gaps on the page without noticing. Ask someone new to the products to find the model with the extra part and take it through checkout. Where do they pause? What do they ask? Then put the listing, cart and receipt side by side. Check the model name and what’s included at each step. You can repeat that same check after the next edit.

A buyer asks whether the attachment is included. Which change helps?

Show the contents of each box It answers the question where it arises. Repeat the variant name in the cart and on the receipt so the choice reaches the warehouse.

Add a discount banner A better price still leaves the buyer guessing which box is coming.

Now suppose the buyer knows what’s in the box but still wonders whether it will fit at home. It’s a different hesitation. Another photograph of the box won’t settle it.

What changes a buying decision?

Behavioural economics looks at how feelings, limited attention, past experience and the way information is presented affect a decision. A buyer weighing price and quality might also be thinking, “What if I choose wrong?” or “Has anyone fitted this in a space like mine?” Those questions can matter as much as the price.

Six behavioural patterns and a store test for each
PatternWhat it meansWhat to try in the store
Loss aversionWhat it meansIn many situations, a loss weighs more heavily in a decision than an equivalent gain.What to try in the storeShow the actual warranty and exchange terms beside the buy button so shoppers know their options if they pick the wrong model.
AnchoringWhat it meansThe first price we see can become a reference point for judging the next ones.What to try in the storeCompare the prices and features of the entry, mid-range and premium models side by side so buyers can see what the extra money buys.
FramingWhat it meansPeople can read the same offer differently depending on how it is expressed, even when they pay the same amount.What to try in the storeShow the same discount as a percentage in one version and as money saved in the other. Display the identical final price in both.
Mental accountingWhat it meansWe might mentally set aside money for a renovation and treat it differently from our day-to-day shopping budget.What to try in the storeGroup sets by renovation budget and show the total price of each set so buyers can compare it with what they’ve set aside.
Social influenceWhat it meansOther people’s experiences can shape a choice, especially when their circumstances resemble our own.What to try in the storePut verified buyers’ reviews and real photos of that model after installation next to its specifications.
Reducing decision frictionWhat it meansIf comparing options or finishing an order is too hard, a shopper may stop there.What to try in the storeMatch one change to the sticking point. Try a simpler model comparison, a size filter or removing an unnecessary checkout field.

What could we test at Boomerang?

My hypothesis is that seeing dimensions, what comes with the set and installation requirements beside the price would clear up some doubts and help buyers finish their orders. For the first test I’d move just the dimensions table. Randomly show visitors either the current page or the same table next to the price. Keep the photos, price and terms of sale the same in both versions.

Fewer questions about fit would be one clue that shoppers found the information. I’d call the change a success if a larger share of visitors completes a purchase, margin holds up and the return rate stays in check. Once the return window closes, compare the margins and the share of orders returned in both groups. That gives us a way to judge whether the change helped both the buyer and the store.
