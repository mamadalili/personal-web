STRATEGY & DECISIONS

Strategy Means Clear Choices

One shopper wants a tool today. Another needs help with an unusual job. When both need the same salesperson, the shop’s strategy shows up in the promise it keeps and the work it puts on hold.

“Be the best” does not settle the choice

Two people message a tool shop at about the same time. One needs a standard tool delivered today. The other can’t tell if a tool will fit their machine and wants advice before buying. Only one person is free to pack an order or check the fit. Which promise can the shop make first?

Michael Porter draws a distinction between doing work better and choosing a strategy. If the salesperson can look up model numbers faster, both shoppers benefit. But a faster lookup still leaves a choice if the fit needs checking just as the carrier is about to leave. Which buyer did the shop set itself up to serve?

The Harvard Business School guide connects what a business offers customers to the activities that make it possible. You can see the connection in the shop. Same-day delivery needs stock ready for dispatch. Advice needs someone who has both the information and the time to check it.

Choose the store’s promise

Each tab gives a different reason for shopping here. Pick one and see what the shop would need in place before the salesperson could say yes.

Quick selection

The buyer wants a standard option

A shopper needs to choose quickly from a few standard tools. Give them the differences that matter. If the next request needs a long consultation, decide whether the shop offers an appointment, charges separately or refers it to someone equipped to help.

A specific problem

The buyer needs help with a difficult use

The buyer sent the machine specifications. Someone needs to check the fit, explain the recommendation and record any limitation. If the technical colleague can’t do that today, allow for the delay when you make the offer.

Custom work

The buyer wants an unusual option

This customer needs a size the shop doesn’t usually carry. Before accepting the order, check what the supplier can provide, who needs to approve it and which part of the delivery time is still unknown.

A choice brief in working language

Take one customer request into the meeting. Write what the salesperson can promise now, whose work it depends on and where they need to say “Let me check.” If the next order falls outside those terms, the salesperson should know who has the authority to accept it.

When a customer asks for something new

If the same request keeps coming in, discuss it with the person who would do the work. If you decide to make it part of the service, change the time you allocate and the promise on the listing together.

Doing it better does not replace choosing

Fewer packing mistakes and quicker replies are worthwhile improvements. A rival can learn the same packing routine. Now suppose the shop wants to serve people who need an ordinary tool at short notice. That choice ties together the items it stocks, how much it keeps on hand and when it hands parcels to the carrier.

A shop for the specialist buyer would ask about the machine model before selling and allow time to check the answer. That buyer may be glad to wait if the check saves them ordering the wrong tool. Both shops could do good work. Their customers would be able to count on them for different things.

If a competitor copies one action, is the strategy gone?

A rival can copy “We’ll help you find the right tool” today. Answering the buyer takes trained staff, specifications, access to the supplier and time to check. It’s harder to reproduce all the work behind the claim.

Make the trade-off visible in the room

A supplier offers more brands. If the shop’s promise is dependable stock on a few common tools, where will the shelf space and purchasing money come from? If stocking new models means running out of the common one, the customer who needs it today feels the cost. You can now put the appeal of the offer and what you would give up on the same page.

Make the choice usable for a new salesperson. They need to know which models should be ready, which requests take investigation and when they can give a firm delivery time. If the decision only exists in the manager’s presentation, the next buyer may hear the same incompatible promises.

For the next proposal, ask which current promise it would help the shop keep, and which order might have to wait longer if you accept. “More sales” still leaves the salesperson without an answer when both customers are on the line.

Test the fit among activities

Compare the promise with what the team is measured on. A salesperson asked to give careful advice but judged only on call volume has a good reason to hurry on to the next call. A warehouse asked for same-day delivery can’t recover the lost time if nobody knows when the supplier will deliver the tool.

For the first shopper, that might mean offering same-day delivery only on items ready to go. For the second, it might mean reserving time specifically for technical checks. Afterwards, find out whether each buyer got what the salesperson promised and whether the shop could afford the time and cost of keeping its word.

Leave the meeting with a decision

Read the two buyers’ messages again at the end of the meeting. Can the salesperson give each one an honest answer about what they’ll receive and when? If sales and the warehouse still disagree about one reply, you know which choice remains open.

Sources & methodology

The Porter reference is limited to the distinction between operational improvement and strategy. The HBS guide discusses business choices. The tool shop is a fictional example.
