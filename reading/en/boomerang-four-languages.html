Cross-border commerce on a four-language site

At Boomerang

For Boomerang’s international business, we built a four-language site separate from the online store. A buyer could browse a gallery, pick a model and ask, “What would you quote for this quantity, shipped here?” That question shaped the design, the copy in each language and my decision about Persian search.

Why build a separate site for international buyers?

One of my first jobs at Boomerang was to work out what kind of site we needed. In the Boomerang Kala store, a shopper added a product to the cart and paid through the domestic payment gateway. For international buyers we wanted a gallery. Once someone found a model they liked, they needed to be able to request a quote from that page and get the inquiry to our sales team.

That changed both the page design and how I’d judge a campaign. Say an ad takes a buyer to a particular model. They should see its photos and specs there, then be able to send an inquiry without having to look up the model name again. In the store I can judge an ad by paid orders. On the international site I’d first ask how many inquiries sales could follow up came through. How many became orders is a separate question.

Four languages built into the site

I wanted people to be able to read the site without relying on their browser to translate it. With help from AI we prepared a separate content document for each of the four languages, Persian, English, Arabic and Russian. Visitors chose their language on the site. The inquiry form raises a different issue. You can ask which language a buyer wants to use, but that won’t tell you where the order needs to go.

A buyer on the English site asks for a quote on several units. What do you need to estimate delivery?

Browser language Someone can read English and take delivery in any number of countries. The browser setting gives sales no delivery address to price against.

Delivery destination Yes. Two buyers can both write in English while one wants delivery to Muscat and the other to Yerevan. Ask for the destination city and country along with the model and quantity.

To check the experience, open a model and switch languages halfway through. Are you still looking at the same product? Can you tell which model the form refers to and which language sales should reply in?

Checking speed at each stage

The four-language site didn’t need the domestic payment gateway or the store’s plugins. I expected that to make it lighter. Even so, speed became one of the biggest challenges. After each stage of development we checked page loading again to see where a new addition might be slowing someone down.

In a gallery the photograph is part of the buying decision. Try opening a model on your phone. When can you actually see the main photo? Can you tap the inquiry button and start typing while the other pictures are still loading? Those two checks help you decide whether to look at the images or the form first.

Would the Persian gallery compete with the store?

Yes, that was a real possibility. We had different keyword plans for the two sites, but model names and many of the phrases Persian-speaking shoppers used overlapped. Someone searching for a product to buy could land on the gallery and its inquiry form. The store was a better destination for that shopper.

I set the Persian version of the four-language site to noindex and focused our SEO work on English, Arabic and Russian. Visitors could still choose Persian on the site.
