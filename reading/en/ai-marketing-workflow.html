AI Marketing Team: Fully Automated, Working for Us

A buyer asks whether a bottle leaks. If AI turns that question into an ad promising a leakproof bottle, where should someone catch it? Follow one customer question from research through the draft, review and report.

When full automation serves the work

Chris’s guide imagines a working day in which one tool finds what customers are saying, another drafts copy and another prepares the report. A shared place for the handoffs could save someone from chasing each file. Before one tool’s draft becomes another’s input, though, the team needs to know what that file actually establishes.

Take a hypothetical bottle. Under its photo, a customer asks, “Will this leak in my bag?” A tool can find the question and write a draft. The question itself tells us nothing about whether the cap seals properly. Who would catch that gap if the draft became an ad? We’ll follow the bottle through the work an AI agent can hand to the next person.

The shop and bottle are a teaching example. Follow the buyer’s question through the map to the report and see where someone might need to send work back. It’s an exercise in designing the work, with no sales figures or measured result from a deployed team.

Start with customer evidence, not a content command

Chris starts with public customer conversations and examples of what competitors have published. One person asking about a wet bag gives you a reason to ask the product team about the bottle cap. You don’t yet know how many buyers share that worry. If a competitor advertises a leakproof bottle, the ad alone tells you neither whether it is leakproof nor how well the ad sold.

In the research note, keep the link, the bottle model and the buyer’s concern about carrying it in a bag together. If you found only this one question, say so. The next person now knows which cap to ask the product owner about. The answer might belong on the product page or in a support guide. An advertising claim still needs evidence. The illustration shows that gap using a fictional product.

The guide suggests asking a simulated customer to score a draft. If the tool says it can’t tell whether the bottle can lie sideways in a bag, that’s a useful editing question. Its score still comes from a simulation. Label it as suggested editorial feedback and keep actual customer responses after publication in a separate record.

The shared workspace needs an inspectable memory

Chris proposes keeping customer notes, ideas, weekly plans and drafts where the next person can find them. Compare two possible notes. “Improve the bottle headline” leaves tomorrow’s writer guessing. “Removed ‘leakproof’ because the specification covers only upright use” tells them exactly which document to reopen.

The bottle copywriter needs a way back from the draft to the customer’s question and the product owner’s answer. After publication, whoever writes the report needs a link to the version customers actually saw. A folder full of undated drafts makes it hard to work out which promise reached them. Those links are part of the workflow you need to set up and check.

Move through the jobs

Select a job to see its input, output, dependency and human check. This is a redesigned workflow from the source, not a record of a deployed system.

In this map, “fully automated” means you don’t have to copy the bottle question from file to file yourself. A tool can collect the question and its link, prepare suggested wording and bring the published version into a report. If the leak test is missing, someone still has to decide what the team can say and whether this draft is worth publishing.

What moves between the roles

A draft and a release are different states

After the plan is approved, the writer and the person making the image are working on the same promise. Suppose the words say “when upright” but the photograph shows the bottle lying in a bag. What would a buyer take from that? Before release, click through from the ad and check the picture, description, price and contact route. Different promises along the way mean the draft needs another pass.

The draft says “safe to carry in a bag.” Whoever handles approval needs the original question, the verified specification for that model and the page an ad would send people to. If the specification only covers an upright bottle, the draft needs to go back before anyone guesses about a bottle in a bag. Now it’s clear what clicking approve would mean.

External contact is not just another content task

While the bottle reply stays in the team folder, you can revise it several times. Once it’s sent, a real person reads it as a message from the shop. In this exercise, the tool finds relevant questions and drafts a reply. The shop’s representative checks whether contact is appropriate, whether sending is permitted and whether the wording fits this person. Platform rules and local requirements apply to the actual send.

B2B

A specific problem, a restrained contact

For a business buyer, start by saying why the offer fits that particular company. A tool can organize public information and draft a short note. The sender should be able to explain why this company, why now and what permission they have. The message volume in Chris’s example isn’t a reason to start sending automatically to a fresh list.

B2C

Inspect discovery and the landing path first

For a bottle buyer, a product-page explanation or a creator’s video may be more helpful than a direct message. Follow the buyer’s route. Where do they see the promise, and after the click, how do they find out if the bottle suits them? The tool can give you several drafts. Check budget, image rights and the promise on the destination page before choosing one to release.

A report needs to answer “what happened?”

A list of what the team made this week helps people coordinate. The shop manager may want to know whether buyers found an answer about carrying the bottle. Counting posts and emails won’t settle that. Decide which behaviour you can actually observe after release. There are no sales figures here from the team in Chris’s guide on which to base a growth or return chart.

Once the bottle copy is live, keep a link to that version. In the report, record what you actually observed and over what period, such as the visits recorded for that page. If you can’t trace visits to orders, or don’t know whether sales were recorded at all, say so beside the count. Next time you can compare the same measure and see which question is still open.

When should this become a numeric chart?

Suppose you want to compare buying enquiries from two weeks. Define a relevant enquiry, say where it was recorded and check how many days of data each week contains. Then you can compare the counts and mark missing days. A score assigned to a draft can’t fill a gap in the enquiry records.

Where does a small workable version begin?

Even if you handle marketing yourself, you can follow that one bag question to the end. Find evidence about this bottle model. Choose one message and a page where buyers can read more. Ask for a draft, compare it with the evidence and publish the version you’re authorized to use. At the end of the week, reopen that version alongside the data you actually collected to decide what’s worth repeating.

Before repeating the cycle, look at where it stalled. If the writer spent the time searching for the source, make it easier to save the link and model together. If the evidence was ready but nobody could approve release, another writing tool won’t move the draft along. Use the checklist to see what you have for one real question and who can make the decision.

Keep following the message into the page

If the bottle ad is ready, click its link as a buyer would. Can you find the same explanation of the cap? a product page that answers the customer’s question picks up at that point.
