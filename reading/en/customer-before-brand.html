See the Brand Through the Customer

You’re buying a gift. If it’s wrong for the recipient, how do they exchange it? That question tells a shop more about what its brand means to the buyer than a vote on a new logo.

IN THIS EXPERIENCE

  • Ask what someone needs to know before choosing a gift.
  • Test a change to the wording or design against the buyer’s original question.
  • To understand a repeat purchase, ask the buyer why they came back.

Start with the customer’s experience

You’ve found a gift you like. One thing is keeping you from ordering. If the gift doesn’t suit the recipient, can they exchange it themselves, or will you have to arrange everything? The shop’s promise to “be there for you” now has a very specific meaning. You need a name to contact and something you can pass on with the gift.

The opening of the Harvard Business Review article asks us to consider the value of a customer relationship. Take a recent order that involved support, with personal details removed. Where did the buyer have to repeat a question? Did someone give an answer that differed from the ad? That gives a brand discussion a particular moment to examine.

A separate MSI research abstract considers how to relate marketing spend to customer value. You don’t need to put a price on this relationship to learn from the gift order. You can already find out what the shop promised and whether the buyer could use that promise.

A situation and a better question

Before buying

What remains uncertain?

Ask a gift buyer to explain the exchange to the recipient. If they have to guess who needs to keep the receipt, you’ve found a specific gap in the buying information.

After buying

Who carries the promise forward?

The recipient has sent a request and heard nothing. Ask support to find it. The email might be friendly and still leave both sides without a way to tell what happened next.

Buying again

Do we know why they return?

The buyer thanked support. To understand what happened next, ask permission to discuss their next gift purchase. They may have meant every word of their thanks and still chosen another shop for an entirely different reason.

Write a decision brief for the change

Give the team a brief they can picture: someone buying a gift should be able to explain the exchange process to the recipient before paying. The designer knows which information needs to be visible. Support knows which question to answer. Both can sit beside a buyer and see whether the page does the job.

Visual design can be part of the answer

If the exchange terms are too small to read on a phone, a visual change makes sense. Ask the same question before and after the edit and listen to the answer the buyer can now find.

The unit of analysis is the relationship

The gift arrives and it’s the wrong size. In a sales report, the order is complete. For the two people trying to arrange an exchange, there’s still quite a bit to do. When you talk about the value of a customer relationship, one useful question is what this part of the experience gives either person to come back for.

Follow each handover to a new person. How much of the story does the customer have to tell again? They might have a request number and still have to find the receipt and describe the item. That effort doesn’t appear in the sales total. The order history can show you where the repetition actually happened.

Does every repeat purchase signal loyalty?

Someone might come back because this is the only shop with the item they need. Ask them why they chose it. They might still have a complaint about the first order that you can’t see in the order count.

Customers do not live inside one brand

A gift buyer may have visited several shops, has a budget and may be unsure of the right size. Inside the shop, it’s natural to think in terms of the brands you stock. Outside it, the search is for a gift that won’t cause trouble if the size is wrong. Ask what the buyer compared before reaching this page.

Their answer may point you to a size guide, a plain account of a product limitation or a delivery date they can rely on. If the less expensive option suits them better, saying why helps them choose. Whether this conversation earned their trust or brought them back is something you can only find out later.

With permission, walk through someone’s next choice, whether or not they returned. Jot down what the buyer actually told you, what appears in the order history and which possible explanation still needs evidence.

End the brand meeting with a decision

The team now has a question worth discussing. A buyer who hasn’t returned might have disliked the material, struggled with the exchange or simply had no other gift to buy. Which explanations can you check against their account or the order history? The absence of a second sale leaves all three open.

If you decide to put exchange instructions in the parcel, give someone the job of checking them with support and agree which orders will include them. You can then see whether the recipient manages to file the request without calling the buyer. If you follow up about the experience, give the customer a choice about hearing from you.

One revision and one piece of evidence

After the edit, ask what message the buyer would put in with the gift. If they can now explain the exchange in their own words, you’ve learned something about this purchase. Whether they come back for another gift is a question for a later order.
