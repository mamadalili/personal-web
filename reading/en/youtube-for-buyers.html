The Most Valuable YouTube Guide

In Persian and English

The studio tour gets plenty of views. The café owner still wants to know whether a barista can reach the apron pocket during a shift. That question gives a workwear studio its next video.

  • Track a view, a sample request and an order separately.

Listen to the person who has to place the order

Imagine you sew aprons for cafés. Someone comments on your studio tour to say they love the colour. Later, a café manager calls. Can a barista pull a cloth from the pocket with one hand? Both are telling you something. The second question is precise enough to film an answer this afternoon.

François suggests making videos for the kind of customer you want more of. Here that person buys uniforms for an entire shift. Pull three things from their last call: the decision they faced, the risk on their mind and the words they actually used. “Will the pocket catch?” already sounds like a title.

“Before I order these for everyone, let me try one during a shift.”

Three different reasons to press play

A first-time café owner might never have thought about pocket placement. A video showing where an apron catches during a shift could get their attention. Someone already dealing with it will learn more from two pocket designs tested side by side. Someone who has chosen a design wants to see how a sample arrives and what they can change before a team order. That gives the channel three distinct assignments.

Try sorting your last ten uploads into those groups. If they all bring in newcomers and none addresses a sample order, the empty column tells you something a total view count can’t. Make the video your salesperson would actually send after that call.

Put the cover’s promise on camera

Our working title is “Which apron pocket can you use one-handed?” One possible cover shows the whole apron on a mannequin. Another shows a barista reaching for the cloth. The second adds information to the title: you can already see what the test will involve.

If your video is eligible for YouTube Studio’s A/B test, try these two ideas with the same title. YouTube uses watch time to choose a winner, and a test can be inconclusive. A minor lettering change wouldn’t answer the question we have here: does the audience come for a look at the garment or to see how it works?

What belongs in the first shot?

Open at the espresso machine. The barista has one hand occupied and reaches for the cloth with the other. Let that attempt play out. Try the second pocket under the same conditions. Now there’s a reason to explain the seam. Would a left-handed barista get the same result? An untested detail makes an honest follow-up.

The editor sends three openings. Which one delivers what the title promised?

A mannequin and a rundown of colours Useful for a colour guide. The viewer who clicked for the pocket test still hasn’t seen a hand use it.

A barista trying both pockets at the machine Yes. The movement shows what differs. Once you have seen it, the stitching detail has a purpose.

The founder describing how many clients the studio has Experience can build trust. For this particular question, a short demonstration provides more direct evidence.

After publication, open the audience retention report and revisit that shot. YouTube says a dip can indicate skipping or leaving. Watch those seconds again. Perhaps the counter hides the hand. Give the editor a time-specific note: “Stay on the close-up from the moment the hand goes in until the cloth comes out.” That’s an edit you can see and later evaluate.

Keep the customer’s question attached to the footage

If you hear the question on a sales call and someone else shoots the video, send a short note that day. Write the question at the top of the production card, list both samples underneath, and name the person bringing them to the shoot. The editor now knows which movement must survive the cut. A paraphrased question is enough to brief the team.

François assigns one owner to each stage. In a two-person studio, one person can check the answer and prepare the garments while the other films and edits. Decide who will try the sample request on a phone and who will review the enquiries next week. If the same handoff keeps failing, you have a specific job to get help with.

Give the buyer somewhere useful to go

The barista has finished the test. “Try a sample on your own shift” follows naturally. Put a direct link in the description and explain where to find it in the video. The destination should answer the next questions: sizing, sample terms and when to expect a reply. Sending people to a home page to hunt for a contact form adds work at the moment they are ready to act.

Give this upload its own tracked link. Record which requests came through it and which were actually from cafés. Then follow those requests through to paid orders. A click, a sample request and a paid order are three different events. A manager who watches and phones later may never use the link. Keep their answer to “How did you hear about us?” as a separate clue. Turning Search Traffic into Sales follows that same gap from a search result.

Choose one change for the next shoot

Suppose people watched the experiment, yet café managers haven’t asked for samples. Before a reshoot, follow the link on your phone and finish the form. Maybe the page never says an individual sample is available. If cafés decide after several shifts, give the newest requests time to mature. One quiet week can’t settle the sales question.

Take one of your own uploads to the next review. Read its title aloud, play the opening on mute, then follow the link on a phone. Mark the first point at which the customer’s question goes unanswered. Give that change an owner and a date to look again. Sometimes the whole meeting earns its keep by getting you one better close-up.
