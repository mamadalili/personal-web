Data Matters When Decisions Change

A driver is about to leave the warehouse with orders still waiting. Who needs to hear about them while there’s time to do something for the customer?

IN THIS EXPERIENCE

  • Before building a report, ask its reader about the last time they had a choice to make.
  • A parcel alert before the van leaves serves a different purpose from an end-of-day count.
  • Record what you knew when you made the call. Add the delivery outcome when it comes in.

Which decision should change?

The van is ready to leave when the dispatcher spots a few parcels by the door. If she can find out whether there’s room, she can still ask the driver to take them. The afternoon report will count those parcels just as accurately, but by then the van will be back. That stretch of time when she can still make a choice is her decision window.

What does the cycling example establish?

The same question of timing comes up in professional cycling. McKinsey’s case study describes how Team Visma | Lease a Bike brings race information together to explore what the coach could do as conditions change. An analyst can look into the details remotely. The coach has to make the call while the race is still unfolding.

Back at the warehouse, the dispatcher has until the van doors close. What would a report need to tell her before then to be worth checking?

Find the missing link in your decision

Which question remains unanswered?

Open a question to see how you’d investigate the count, the timing of the alert or permission to change the load.

In a race, arrival time matters

A coach following a race from a car has a next move to consider. An accurate account of a situation the riders have already passed can help the team review it later. What would have helped the coach in the moment? The cycling case makes that difference between reviewing a choice and making it especially easy to see.

At the warehouse, one report counts the parcels left behind at the end of the day. Another identifies which parcels are ready while the driver is still there. Both have a reader. Give the first to the person planning tomorrow’s dispatch and the second to the person standing beside the van.

Does the case prove AI caused the team to win?

The people describing the project helped deliver it. Their account tells us how the team uses the information. To estimate the tool’s contribution to a win, we’d need to know how the team would have performed under comparable conditions without it. The case provides no such comparison.

Find the smallest useful signal

Consider the parcels by the door again. What would last month’s order total tell the dispatcher about loading them today? She needs a much smaller set of facts. Which parcels are ready? How much space is left? Would the extra stop delay other deliveries? Each answer could actually change what she asks the driver to do.

Even “one space left” needs context. Did the driver just confirm it, or did the software infer it from this morning’s manifest? Put the source and time beside the number. If the driver hasn’t replied, “capacity unknown” gives the dispatcher a reason to call before promising a delivery.

Pick a report from your own work. Who could have done something differently if it had arrived an hour earlier? If nobody comes to mind, ask someone who reads it to walk you through the last time they needed it.

Learning closes after the action

When the van returns, add the outcome to the shift notes. Perhaps the driver made the delivery because the route happened to be quiet. Or the dispatcher made a reasonable call and the recipient wasn’t there. The outcome helps you ask a better question if you keep later events separate from what was known at dispatch.

Lay a few shifts’ notes side by side. You might find that parcel status always arrives in time, yet the dispatcher still calls the driver on every run to check capacity. Now you can name the missing piece of information, where to put it and who would use it.

Leave evidence for the next decision

Before tomorrow’s van leaves, write one sentence about why you sent a parcel or held it back, using what you know at the time. Add the delivery outcome on the next line when it comes in. Next time you’ll have a way to judge which single piece of earlier information would have changed your call.

A proposed first deliverable

Keep a slip by the dispatcher’s desk. One entry might read, “I sent the parcel. The driver confirmed there was room. Delivery at the destination is still unknown.” Updating that slip when news comes in is easier than reconstructing the whole decision from memory.
