When Work Outgrows Team Capacity

The team already owes someone work on Thursday. An urgent request lands. If you say yes to both, whose time will it take? Follow one request through the conversations needed to change a commitment.

IN THIS EXPERIENCE

  • Name what new work displaces.
  • Delegate decision authority with the task.
  • Show the capacity gap upward with evidence and negotiate scope or time.

Turn overload into a decision

The HBR article opens with a legal manager at a financial firm. Several acquisitions have brought in more contracts and regulatory work than the team can handle. The manager takes the rest home and works nights and weekends. Deadlines may still look fine. What disappears from view is the time the manager was hired to spend on longer-term decisions.

Picture a marketing team that promised a revised product page on Thursday. Sales now needs a proposal written by the same day. Saying yes to both leaves one question open. Who has time to write it? The manager could move the page deadline, deliver part of the proposal later or bring in help. Each choice changes a promise someone is relying on.

Before trimming the work, find out what is genuinely open to negotiation. A check needed for safety, a legal duty or a customer’s rights still needs a place in the plan. Look for choices among the remaining commitments.

Four real responses to new work

Keep the sales proposal in mind as you open the options. Under “Reduce scope,” sales might decide it only needs prices and terms for the meeting. Examples can follow. Now try moving the work. If the product page slips, who needs to agree to the new date?

Where does the new request fit?

Each option gives you a question for the team and the requester.

For Thursday, you might bring in help and prepare a shorter proposal. Sales and the page owner both need to know what they’ll receive and which date has changed. The strategy article follows that question through the promise a business makes to its customers.

Have two different conversations

Ask the writer which part of the page would miss Thursday if they switched to the proposal today. Then take the choice to the person who sets priorities. “We can have the proposal ready on Thursday if we move the page deadline” gives them something to accept or reject. The tabs show what each conversation can tell you.

With the team

What can pause or shrink?

Ask the writer, “If you switch to the proposal, what part of the page will be left? Which checks do you still need before anything goes out?” Record what can wait and who will feel the effect.

Upward

Show options and consequences

Tell the decision maker, “We can have prices and terms by Thursday if the examples follow the meeting. We’d need help to finish both the full proposal and the page.” That gives them a specific offer to accept or change.

What if Thursday’s meeting can’t move?

Treat Thursday as fixed and ask sales which parts of the proposal the meeting needs. If those still won’t fit, show the remaining work and the consequence of delay to the person who can change priorities or bring in help.

Take decision rules out of the manager’s head

Even with the deadline settled, the writer may wait for a manager to approve every edit. HBR suggests sharing the reasoning behind decisions. In this proposal, for example, which terms have sales already agreed to, and which changes need to go back to the person responsible for them?

On the same sales proposal, the writer could rearrange the wording or choose a shorter example. If an edit would lead the customer to expect a different price or delivery date, it goes back to whoever approved those terms. The writer can keep editing without quietly changing the customer’s deal.

For one actual edit, write down who can make it, which approved wording they can refer to and where they need to ask for help. After the proposal goes out, find out whether those three lines helped or left the writer waiting.

Delegating tasks without authority leaves the queue

If the manager only says “You write it,” the writer may come back about every sentence. Try giving them the decision too. “Shorten the copy and choose the example yourself. If the price or promised delivery date changes, check with sales before it goes to the customer.” The writer can see where their authority ends.

Task only

I execute, but ask at every turn

The writer prepares the copy but has no idea whether they can shorten an example. The draft waits for an answer, even though the manager thinks the work is already in someone else’s hands.

Task + authority

Boundaries are clear; exceptions rise

The writer can change the example and leave a reason in the draft. If a sales term changes, they know who to ask before sending it. The manager can give attention to the choices the writer genuinely can’t make.

If an AI assistant helps with the proposal, the same boundary matters. May it shorten a sentence? May it send the customer a new price? The article on AI agents looks at those permissions and who checks the result.

Empathy is not an unsupported promise

The customer meeting may genuinely have to happen on Thursday. Tell the team that and ask what must be ready for it. If pricing is still open, find the person who can confirm it and when you need their answer. The deadline is real, and the team can work towards it without guessing on the customer’s or manager’s behalf.

When does “just this week” become routine?

Pick a day to revisit the arrangement. Find out what happened to the deferred work, what went wrong and who stayed late. If the same colleague works late every Thursday, reopen the decision about the commitments themselves.

One week, one explicit decision

Take one live request to the next meeting. By the end you should be able to write one sentence for the people involved, such as “Prices and terms are due Thursday, and we’ve agreed a new date with the page owner.” Read it again next week. Did the work you chose actually move, or did someone stay late to keep both promises?

An urgent project arrives while the team is full. Which response creates a real decision?

If the team loses time to the same rework every week, the article on recurring friction follows that work to the next person. If the proposal is ready and just needs a decision, the leader’s calendar looks at it from the other side of the invitation.
