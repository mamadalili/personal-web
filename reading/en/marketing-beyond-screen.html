When Marketing Leaves the Screen

A visitor wants to know whether a shelf will fit her kitchen. Give her a tape measure and a sample at the booth. What could you learn from what she does next?

IN THIS EXPERIENCE

  • A tape measure and two samples give someone choosing a shelf a reason to stop.
  • Count seeing the logo, trying a sample and requesting a guide separately. They show different behaviour.
  • A link on the take-home card can identify some visits. Finding out what prompted a purchase takes other evidence.

Why should people welcome your presence?

A visitor at a home show stops between two kitchen shelves. “Which one is shallower?” A booth with a big logo and a stack of brochures leaves her looking elsewhere for the answer. If she can handle both samples and measure them, she has a reason to stay. That small, hypothetical encounter gives us somewhere to start designing an event.

In the available excerpt from an EMARKETER discussion, the guests talk about what both sides get from taking part. Physical events have been around for a long time. The interesting question is what a brand can add to the visitor’s time there. The discussion doesn’t tell us how the returns from events in general compare with online ads.

What will the participant actually do?

Choose the experience’s purpose

Pick the reason someone came. Each choice changes what you need in the booth and what you can check afterwards.

Make the next step traceable

Put a link to the measuring guide on the take-home card. In Google Analytics, UTM parameters are labels added to a web address to identify a campaign that sent a visit. A visit through the booth link gives you a recorded visit. A submitted measurement or an order needs its own record.

Does that link measure the event’s full effect?

A visitor may keep the card, search for the shop a week later and buy in person. The link report won’t capture that whole route. If she says she first saw the shelf at the booth, record that as her account. Estimating extra purchases caused by the event would require a suitable comparison.

Physical attention differs from an impression

The EMARKETER discussion features people from Atmosphere TV and Sparks, and Atmosphere TV sponsored the episode. Read the ideas in that commercial context. At our hypothetical booth, a smaller question is more useful than declaring one medium better. What can the buyer find out more easily with the shelf in front of her?

Seeing the shop’s name across the hall, stopping at the table and trying a shelf are different behaviours. If the team wants to know which dimension confuses buyers, someone needs to be there when they take a measurement and ask their question. Counting people in the aisle can’t do that job.

Must every experience be large and expensive?

You don’t need an exhibition stand for every question. A sample on a shop table and a few minutes with a fitter might be enough for someone to see why a cabinet door catches. Size and budget should fit the help the team can actually offer.

What can B2B gatherings teach us?

At a trade gathering, someone may come specifically to meet a person who has handled the same problem. A home buyer could use that kind of encounter too. Someone who brings a cabinet photo could spend a few minutes with a fitter and leave knowing which measurement to check before ordering. Both people know what the conversation is for.

When a queue forms, it’s tempting to collect every phone number and promise a later consultation. First see how many questions the team can actually handle. Give the others a guide they can use without handing over contact details. If someone wants more help, let them ask for it.

The shelf booth and the fitter are examples for this design exercise. For a different event, try asking what someone could do after a few minutes there that they found difficult before arriving.

Measurement should show its own limits

Choose a main aim before the event. For this booth it could be that visitors can take the right measurements at home. At the end of the exercise, ask where they’ll put the tape. If several forget the clearance for the door, you have a specific revision to make to the guide. Scan counts wouldn’t reveal it.

Keep what the team saw distinct from what it suspects. “Visitors asked about door clearance” is an observation. “That question kept them from buying” is an explanation that needs checking. At the next event, try a clearer guide and watch where people pause this time.

Write the invitation before booking space

Write the invitation before booking the space. “Bring a photo of your cabinet. We’ll use two samples and a tape measure to see which shelf fits, and you can take the measuring guide home.” Now you can cost that promise, staff the table and judge whether a visitor has a reason to come.
