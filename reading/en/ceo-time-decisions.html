A Leader’s Calendar Is for Decisions

Three meetings about one order, and the customer is still waiting. Before finding another slot, work out what the team needs to decide and whether you’re the person they need in the room.

Start with the week’s decision

An hour in this week’s calendar says “customer follow-up.” Sales promised a date that delivery says it can’t meet. You heard the update last time, and the same people are invited again. Before accepting, ask what answer the customer needs after this meeting and who has the authority to give it.

Fast Company asked executives about their time and discussed the difference between how people describe a day and what observation shows. You can look for that gap in this meeting. It might be labelled “customer” while the whole hour goes on reading updates and nobody gets to ask the person handling the order why it’s late.

What does the invitation need?

Inspect a real invitation

Is the invitation about a decision, a question or an update? Choose one to see what to ask before accepting.

Separate attendance from authority

Atlassian’s DACI approach distinguishes the person moving a decision along, the final decision maker, contributors and people who need to hear the outcome. For this order, who will get a workable date from delivery, and who can offer it to the customer? The leader might only need to hear what was agreed. Atlassian’s DACI guide.

Who will get back to the customer?

Write down the decision, why it was made and who will pass it on. If you still can’t offer a date, record which piece of information is missing and who will get it before the next conversation.

The described calendar differs from the lived one

Nine CEOs told Fast Company how they spent their time. Most described time with senior teams and customers. Some set aside room to think or try an idea. The article sets those accounts beside earlier work that recorded executive time more systematically. Recalling a week from memory and recording what actually happened can give quite different pictures.

Open a week you’ve already worked. Beside the late-order meeting, note the decision you expected, what you actually discussed and which question came back to you later. A short call to the person making the delivery might have turned up something an hour of updates didn’t. The calendar label and duration won’t tell you that.

Should you copy other CEOs’ percentages?

Companies differ in size and in what they face this week. An executive’s account might prompt you to ask when you last heard directly from a customer or where your team needs authority. A proportion of someone else’s calendar won’t answer either question for you.

Which decision truly needs you?

Picture the meeting without you. If you only need to hear about the delay, you can receive the update afterwards. If honouring this order would delay a promise to another customer and only you may choose between them, the team has a concrete reason to ask for your time. Establish that before gathering everyone.

If you hand the choice to the delivery lead, agree on its limits. What cost can they accept? Who needs to hear if the second customer’s date also changes? That gives someone close to the work room to make a reversible choice and leaves the leader available for a decision that changes a bigger commitment.

Look at three invitations for next week. One might belong with a colleague closer to the work. Another may need a question and some evidence before it’s worth holding. For the third, identify the choice that actually calls for your presence.

Protected time needs a difficult question

The executives described different ways of reflecting, including reading, talking with a team and building something new. Look for the question that keeps getting crowded out of your week. Perhaps you’ve always blamed this kind of delay on staffing but never watched how sales offers a date to the buyer. That gap gives you something worth setting time aside for.

You don’t have to announce a decision at the end of every quiet hour. If you discover you need to compare two orders or ask sales about an exception, write that down. Next week you can pick up there, instead of reopening the whole discussion with “Why are we always late?”

Protect a question for reflection

Keep a question from this order for a quiet hour next week. Why did the shop promise a date the delivery team couldn’t meet? Look for a case that challenges your first explanation. You might end the hour without a firm answer but know which colleague to speak to or which record to inspect. That gives the next follow-up a place to start.

Sources & methodology

The executives in Fast Company describe their own time. Their accounts don’t establish how other leaders should allocate their hours. The order and calendar exercise are original examples.
