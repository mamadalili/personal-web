Where Should AI Enter Advertising?

A team has a pile of audience questions. AI could sort them, but what if its draft puts an experience the creator never had into their mouth? That distinction helps you choose a first advertising task to test.

Start with one task

An ad team wants to tell a creator what people keep asking about a product. Reading every question takes time. An AI tool could group similar questions and show an example from each group. A colleague could check the result against the originals. Asking the tool to write about using the product in the creator’s voice would be a different assignment.

Digiday looked at how marketers use AI with influencers and on connected TV, or CTV. For our team, it helps to picture where the output goes next. A question list read around a table and a line of copy about to play on someone’s television need different kinds of review.

Choose an experiment boundary

Where will the output be used?

Pick a task to see who would check the result and what they would check it against.

Keep trust in the handoff

“People asked how long it lasts” might be a fair summary of the questions. “I tried it and it lasted ages” is a claim about personal experience. The creator needs to be able to stand behind that sentence. In the US, the FTC gives guidance on truthful endorsements and making material brand relationships clear. Keep the supporting evidence alongside the version the creator approved. FTC guidance.

The survey maps caution; it is not a verdict

Digiday surveyed more than a hundred marketing professionals in the first quarter of 2026. Respondents reported less AI use in influencer work than in some other channels. Most CTV respondents had yet to adopt it there. Those answers describe a particular group at a particular time. They can’t tell our team how accurately a tool will sort its questions.

Among people already using AI for influencer work, analysis was more common than creating content or contacting people. Consider what happens if the tool groups two questions incorrectly. An analyst can split them before a meeting. Publish that interpretation on the creator’s account and the audience hears it as something the creator personally said.

Does low adoption mean the tool is useless?

The survey can’t explain why every team waited. Some may lack suitable data or find the final review takes too long. Your team can try one assignment and find out which part saves effort and which part adds it.

In creator work, the voice belongs to the creator

Give the creator the question list and let them tell you which things they’ve actually tried. The brief is the short note explaining what the ad needs to cover. A clear brief leaves room for the creator to describe their experience in their own words, revise a line or say that they haven’t tried that feature.

If some questions came in as private messages, check that you have permission for this use before you put them into a tool. When the ad is ready, the person publishing it needs to know where each claim came from, which version was approved and how the audience will learn about the commercial relationship. Disclosure rules depend on the country.

For a first trial, give the creator a brief where each point can be traced back to a question and they can choose whether to answer it. Save their edits so the team can see where the tool misread the material.

CTV has its own creative and measurement limits

Picture the same durability message on TV. A qualification that’s easy to read on a laptop might disappear when you watch from the sofa. Digiday describes uses in analysis, ad placement and creative work, alongside concerns about creative quality and about combining measurements from different places.

Watch and listen to the final version on a TV before it airs. Then decide what you can actually measure about the campaign. You might know production took less time and still have no way to tell which purchases followed from seeing the ad. Keep those findings distinct when you decide whether to try another one.

What survives the experiment?

Go back to the question list you originally needed. For comparable assignments, compare the time it took to prepare and check it with your usual method and with the tool. Which summary was good enough to give the creator? If the grouping worked but the draft exaggerated the answers, you have a reason to keep the tool on the first task.

What made it into the final version?

Which audience question did the final message address, and which line did the reviewer cut? Those answers tell you more about this particular use of the tool than a count of the drafts.
