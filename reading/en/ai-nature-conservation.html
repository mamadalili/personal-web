AI for Nature: From Detection to Action

A camera spots weeds in one corner of a field. What has to happen on the ground before anyone can say that helped nature?

IN THIS EXPERIENCE

  • A marked plant can lead a grower to a patch of weeds. Measuring an effect requires knowing what happened there afterwards.
  • Before installing a camera, check who investigates an alert and whether the equipment can treat that patch alone.
  • A lower bill and a change in what reached the field need different evidence.

Better detection starts a chain

A farm camera marks a few plants in a row of crops. Its software thinks they’re weeds. What can the grower do with that picture? She might need to inspect the row first. Her equipment might only be able to treat the whole field. Until we know which choice the alert makes possible, a count of detected weeds tells us little about the benefit to nature.

Where does the source take the argument?

BCG discusses precision farming, crop protection and alternative ingredients. In each case, someone has to change how they grow, make or buy something before pressure on nature can fall. In our field example, start with the decision about where to spray.

Open the three stages in order. You’ll need different evidence for what the camera saw, what the grower did about it and what changed in the field afterwards.

Inspect the impact chain

Observation: is the signal trustworthy?

Ask someone familiar with local plants to inspect some marked areas and some unmarked ones. The first check catches plants mistaken for weeds. The second can uncover weeds the camera missed. Record the season and location so a result from this field doesn’t turn into a claim about every field.

Action: what changes in the work?

If the plants really are weeds, can the grower treat just that patch? Who tells the operator where it is, and where do they record the amount applied? If the machine can’t isolate a patch, the next step may be a change of practice or a different trial. Processing more pictures won’t address that obstacle.

Outcome: did pressure actually fall?

Give the ecological pressure a measure, such as the amount of a particular chemical applied per hectare. Compare actual use with a suitable plot under the usual practice. A claim about insects or soil needs observations of those outcomes too. Lower input use alone tells us little about the condition of every species on the farm.

Three routes linking ecological impact to business value

Precision farming here means identifying which part of the field needs attention and being able to act there. BCG’s other examples involve different choices. A crop-protection developer wants a promising compound to test. An ingredient buyer wants to know whether an alternative that might require less land will work in the finished product.

Two farm records can tell different stories. An invoice shows what the grower paid. An application log shows which substance reached which field and when. If the price fell, the grower could spend less while applying just as much. Use the record that matches the claim you’re making.

How far does the model take a new product?

A model can help a researcher choose a few compounds to test from a long list. Their effects on the target plant and other species still need testing. Picking a candidate worth investigating is an earlier decision than establishing whether it can be produced, approved and used.

Detecting loss differs from reducing its cause

A similar gap shows up in forest monitoring. An image of changing tree cover can tell a local team where to investigate. Changing harvesting, purchasing or planting practices may require someone else’s authority. Before commissioning a tool, find the person who can both use its information and carry out the next step.

In the ingredient example, a buyer receives a sample that worked in the lab. Can the factory get enough of it at consistent quality? A comparison of land use also needs to account for how much usable product each process produces. Those checks let the buyer and the conservation specialist see where their evidence still runs out.

Use detection, decision, action and outcome to design a trial. To report an actual result, go back to an observation from that project for each step.

Which part of the chain will you change?

Three routes, three kinds of proof

The grower, developer and buyer each face a different decision. Open a route to see which question still needs testing.

Name evidence and ownership before scaling

Before the field trial starts, decide what you’ll compare it with. A similar plot under the usual practice may help if you also consider differences in soil, season and crop. Agree who will log actual inputs, who will check the harvest and what observation would prompt the team to stop.

The grower might first ask how to repair the equipment, and the buyer about crop quality. A conservation group needs to know how the environmental claim will be checked. Bring those questions into the trial design while it can still change. Continued use depends on someone being willing to operate the tool and others being able to examine the result.

Make the next step testable

At the end of the season, return to that first picture. If the camera spotted weeds correctly and the operator treated only that patch, you can document two steps. The third still calls for the input records and the environmental observation you agreed on at the start. Then you can decide about trying another plot.

If all you have are marked pictures, end the report there and name the missing observation. The next person can go to the field to find out what happened after the alert instead of adding more pictures to the count.
