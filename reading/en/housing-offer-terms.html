When Terms Move the Deal Forward

Two offers on the same US home. One takes less cash on closing day. Follow the money to see whether the difference comes from the seller, the mortgage terms or when a cost gets paid.

IN THIS EXPERIENCE

  • Check how the offer changes both cash at closing and later payments.
  • Find out who is contributing to the cost.
  • Locate the agreed amount and conditions in the estimate and final documents.

Read the whole offer

An Associated Press report carried by Fast Company describes sellers in a slow US market offering to cover some buyer costs. That can ease the strain on closing day. What the buyer can afford in the months that follow is a separate question.

Put two offers on the same US home side by side. The price is identical, but one advertises “help with buyer costs.” Start by asking who pays and which charge the money covers. A contribution from the seller and a credit offered by the lender in exchange for a different interest rate can leave the buyer with different obligations.

Beside the offer, jot down who pays, when the money is applied and where you could see its effect in writing. If all you have is a phrase in a listing, you’ve found a question to take to the people handling the transaction.

Separate three terms

Seller credit

A seller contribution

When a seller agrees to cover some closing costs, the contribution may appear as a seller credit, or a particular charge may be listed as paid by the seller. Ask which line reflects the agreement for this offer.

Lender credit

A lender’s pricing tradeoff

In the rate-linked arrangement CFPB describes, a lender covers some upfront costs in exchange for a higher interest rate. Ask the lender for its offer with and without the credit so you can see the difference in the actual rates.

Points

An upfront payment for a lower rate

Discount points are money the buyer pays at closing for a lower interest rate. The reduction depends on the offer. Ask how much you would need at closing without points and how the rate and monthly payment would change.

Move from the label to the document

Start with the Loan Estimate and compare it with the terms you discussed. Receiving that estimate doesn’t mean the loan has been approved. On the final Closing Disclosure, look for Cash to Close, the money needed at closing. Closing Costs covers the charges for completing the transaction. The two figures can differ. Loan Estimate guide and Closing Disclosure guide.

Suppose the seller agreed to cover part of the costs but the cash you need on the day hasn’t fallen. Ask the lender to walk you through the line in question. A different charge may have changed at the same time. Find the actual difference between the documents, and take contract or loan-program questions to the professional handling that part.

Two sellers, different constraints

In the Associated Press report, one seller wanted to move to a smaller home without carrying two mortgages at once. Another hoped to relocate for work but had little equity left to absorb a further price cut. Both wanted to sell, yet their reasons for being able to accept a particular offer were quite different.

Look at the buyer’s side too. Someone may be comfortable with the monthly payment and short of cash on closing day. Someone else may have cash ready now and worry about later payments. A contribution to closing costs or repairs costs the seller money. For the buyer, its value depends on which expense actually changes and when.

Why is the listing price not the whole story?

At the same price, one seller might cover a repair while the other offers flexibility over timing. Check what the buyer would give or receive in each case and which conditions are written into the agreement.

Translate a concession into its mechanism

Give each concession a verb. Does the seller reduce the price, pay a closing charge or offer money towards repairs? Or does the offer describe a lower mortgage rate that depends on terms from the lender? All could be advertised as help for the buyer, yet you would need to check different parts of the paperwork to understand the effect.

Make a column for each of the two offers. Under the price, give the seller contribution, closing charges, cash needed on the day and later payments their own lines. If one says “repair allowance” without an amount or a condition, leave the entry open. To finish the comparison you need that detail from the contract or the professional handling it.

The two columns might add up to the same cash need today. If they differ on the mortgage rate or a repair obligation, you still have two different offers. Use the line where they diverge to form your next question.

Keep the news report separate from your decision

The market report helps explain why a seller might negotiate terms. To value a particular home or assess a mortgage, though, you need its location, condition and the buyer’s circumstances. Even a national average leaves you needing the actual estimate and contract to see what the concession would do for this property.

When someone says the closing costs are taken care of, you can follow up. Where is that written down? Will the lender accept that arrangement for this particular loan? What happens to the agreement if a condition isn’t met? Take each question to the person responsible and compare the final figures with the terms you were given at the start.

One concession does not settle the comparison

An offer requires less cash at closing. Must its total cost be lower?

What would you still need to ask?

Explain the offer in your own words. For example, the seller covers this cost, the lender quotes this rate, and you still need to know what happens if a repair condition falls through. That gives you a specific question and a document to ask about.
