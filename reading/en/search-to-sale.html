Turning Search Traffic into Sales

SEO in the Age of AI

Your espresso machine is leaking. Search turns up three repair shops nearby. Which one gets your call? Following that choice reveals where a business earns a customer and where it loses one.

Whose neighbourhood are you searching from?

The owner searches for “espresso machine repair near me” from the workbench. The shop is near the top of the map. A colleague runs the same search across town and can’t see it at all. They’re both looking at real results. “Near me” means a different place for each person.

Start with the area you actually serve. Check the same query from several fixed locations and record the date. Repeat next month. If you only appear around the workshop, you have a concrete question to investigate. How would a customer two neighbourhoods away know you offer collection there?

Follow the shop’s details across its site, map listing and local directories. Which one still lists the old number? If you have two branches, the west branch page should give its own intake hours, address and phone number. For a workshop in Iran, Google’s list of supported regions rules out setting up a Google Business Profile. Start with your own branch page and local directories where you can correct the listing.

Listen for the decision behind the query

One person types “why is there water under the tank?” They may just want to check whether it is seated properly. Someone searching “De’Longhi espresso repair near me” is probably looking for a place to take the machine. Both visits matter, but the same page can get in the way of one of those decisions.

For the first search, a short guide with a photo of the leak is useful. Explain when to hand the job to a technician. For the second, list the models you accept, your inspection fee and how to drop off the machine. Link the guide to that service page so someone who decides to book can go straight there.

“If I bring it in and it can’t be repaired, do I still pay the inspection fee?”

Compare that imagined customer question with “expert service of the highest quality.” Which gives you something to write? Ask the person at reception to note three recurring questions this week. Answering one precisely can do more for a hesitant customer than another paragraph about the workshop’s history.

What can a customer check before calling?

Open the service page on a phone, one-handed. Do they repair my model? Are they open today? Where do I send the photo? A neighbouring shop might get the call while you are still scrolling. Put a clear action next to the intake details, such as “Send a photo of your machine.” Try it right through to the message arriving at reception.

Trust starts with things a visitor can check. A real photo of the bench and a customer’s note about turnaround time help someone decide. After collection, invite the customer to describe their experience in their own words. If a parts supplier genuinely recommends your workshop, check where its link lands. Paying for links to influence rankings goes against Google’s spam policies.

When the search page answers first

Imagine getting an answer about the leak right at the top of the results. You may skip the guide altogether. Once the machine needs to be opened, though, the next question is different. Who nearby repairs this model and can take it before Saturday? A model list, service area and current intake times suddenly matter.

Google says a page needs to be indexed and eligible to appear with a snippet to be considered as a supporting link in AI Overviews or AI Mode. If your model list lives in a tiny photo, put it in readable text. If the only route to the page runs through a booking form, link it from your services. Inclusion is never guaranteed. For a closer look at what you can actually measure, see measuring AI search.

Traffic went up. What happened to paid work?

In week one the workshop gets 200 search visits, 20 repair enquiries and 10 paid jobs. The next week brings 250 visits. Switch the chart from visits to enquiries and then paid jobs. What impression would you have taken away if the owner had shown you just the traffic report?

After week two, what would you check first?

Publish for more search terms. That may help later. The extra 50 visits still produced 20 enquiries. Find out what those visitors came for first.

Look at the incoming queries and try contacting the shop from the service page. Yes. The extra visitors may have wanted a free guide, or the contact button may be broken. The chart points to a place to investigate. It can’t tell you the cause.

Conclude that reception is closing fewer jobs. Both weeks produced 10 paid jobs from 20 enquiries. There is no drop in that step in these figures.

Compare weeks three and four. Both had 300 visits, yet paid jobs went from nine to fifteen. To explain that, open the intake log. How many people had a model the shop doesn’t service? How many calls went unanswered? Did someone ask for a price and leave, or bring the machine in? A conversion rate narrows the question. The answer is often in those conversations.

Trace one job all the way through this week

Pick one service and one neighbourhood. Search on a phone, open the page and use the contact method you offer. For recent real enquiries, note how the customer found you, what they needed and whether the job went ahead. Record calls straight from the map separately. Those customers never made a website visit.

If several callers asked about the inspection fee, put the answer beside the intake button this week. Write down the date. Next week, listen for whether callers still ask about the fee or have moved on to their next question. That one edit can earn its place on the page in the language you hear at the desk.
