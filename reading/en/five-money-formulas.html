Five Formulas for Understanding Money

Start with 100 units of money. You gain 20% one year and lose 20% the next. Are you back at 100? Start there, then see how time, fees and rising prices change the answer to five everyday money questions.

Before the percentage, check the comparison

Say there are 100 units in your account at the start of the year. By December the balance is higher. Before you work out how much you gained, ask a few questions. Did you put money in during the year? Did a fee come out? What would your first shopping basket of the year cost now? The five calculations in this article answer different parts of that story.

In each tool, change one assumption and watch the number or chart update. Try changing the fee while leaving everything else alone. The figures here are examples for learning the arithmetic. To understand a real offer, you’d need the rate, fees and payment dates that actually apply to it.

100 money units rise 20% in one year, then fall 20% the next. What remains?

A customer wants to pay later and you’re weighing that against cash today? Start with present value. If the balance rose but shopping feels harder, try real return. When an advertised percentage doesn’t look like the gain in the account, look at uneven returns.

Growth works on the existing balance; so do fees

Leave the same 100 units in the account for a year at an assumed 8%. You’ll have 108. Leave the gain there, and next year’s 8% applies to all 108, taking the balance to 116.64. That’s compound growth. The second year adds more than eight units because the first year’s eight were still in the account.

Leave the fee at zero and extend the time in the calculator. Keep the same duration and set the fee to 2%. Look at two different numbers. One is the sum of the fees taken out. The other is the gap between the ending balances. Money taken out in the first year can’t grow in later years, which is why the numbers can differ.

Compare time and fees

Change the amount, rate or duration, then select the fee example. Both paths start with the same capital.

The calculator uses one specific fee rule. A contract might charge a flat amount, take a percentage of the balance or charge only when you trade. When comparing two offers, find the amount each fee applies to and the date it leaves the account.

Why is a constant rate not a promised outcome?

The formula answers an “if” question. If the rate holds and you leave the money there, where do you end up? In practice the rate may change, the balance may fall or you may need to make a withdrawal. If you put in fresh money, its amount and date both belong in the calculation.

The rule of 72 is an estimate, not contract arithmetic

If the same 100 units grew at a steady 8% a year, when would they reach 200? For a rough estimate, divide 72 by 8. That gives about nine years. Compare it with the exact answer in the calculator, then change the rate and see when the shortcut starts to drift.

Compare the estimate with the exact time

Change 8% to 40% and inspect the gap. Try zero too.

Try 40%. Dividing 72 by 40 gives 1.8 years to double, while the exact time in this model is about 2.06 years. The shortcut may serve for a mental estimate. For a particular payment date, look at the exact column and its unit. An annual rate gives years, a monthly rate gives months.

100 today and 100 next year are different comparisons

Now say you’ve done a job. The customer offers 100 units today or a larger amount later. You need a way to express both amounts on the same date. The discount rate is the rate you assume when working back from the later amount to its equivalent today. Move the payment further out in the calculator and watch what happens to its present value.

Put today’s payment and the later promise on one basis

Change the future amount or the payment delay. The difference is a comparison at your selected rate.

Suppose you’re due 1,000 units in a year. At your assumed annual comparison rate of 6%, about 943.40 today would grow to that 1,000. The invoice still says 1,000. The 943.40 gives you a figure you can compare with a payment available today.

Who chooses the discount rate?

Ask what you’re comparing the later payment with and how certain you are it will arrive. Inflation tells you about prices. It doesn’t tell you whether this customer pays on time. If you adjust your comparison rate for a particular risk, check you haven’t also deducted the same effect from the amount due.

Set the invoice beside the bank balance. You made the sale and the customer owes you money, but can you pay a supplier with it today? For that you need the collection date and relevant contract terms. The calculator lets you isolate what the wait does to the comparison.

An average of percentages is not the money path

Follow 100 through three years. A 50% gain takes it to 150. Losing half of 150 leaves 75. A 50% gain on that 75 takes you to 112.50. The simple average of the three annual rates is about 16.67%, yet your total gain across all three years was 12.5%. To get the same 112.50 with a constant rate each year, you’d need an equivalent compound rate of about 4%.

Some figures in the source example don’t agree with its own sequence of returns. That’s why we started with 100 and followed the balance year by year. In the tool, try giving all three years the same rate. Then the simple average and the equivalent compound rate agree.

Change the returns and inspect the balance path

Three annual returns over equal periods, starting from 100 units. The ending balance and both means are shown separately.

Does rearranging the years change the ending balance?

Rearrange the three years. With no deposits or withdrawals, you’re multiplying the same three factors, so the closing balance stays the same. The balances along the way can look very different. Now imagine depositing money just after the first year. That money sees only the returns after it arrives. Your own return depends on the dates of your payments too.

Next time you hear “average return,” ask which periods were averaged, how they were averaged and whether money moved in or out. To see why a loss matters, halve 100. You now have 50, and that 50 must double to get you back to 100. A 50% fall takes a 100% gain to recover.

A larger balance—but how much can it buy?

The balance grew, but your weekly shop got more expensive too. To see how much of the same basket you can now buy, divide the money growth factor by the price growth factor. A 5% increase, for example, means a factor of 1.05. The percentage change left after that comparison is the real return. Use rates for the same stretch of time.

Translate the balance into purchasing power

Start with 5% growth and 4% inflation, then select 28% and 40%. Both are entirely hypothetical.

Subtracting can get close at low rates. With 5% growth and 4% inflation, the exact real gain is about 0.96%, close to one percentage point. Now try 28% growth and 40% inflation. Subtraction gives minus 12%, while the change in purchasing power is about minus 8.57%. The shortcut makes a sizeable difference here.

Read the example in millions of tomans. At an assumed 28% gain, the balance rises from 100 to 128. If the price of the basket rises 40%, that 128 at year-end buys about what 91.43 million tomans bought at the start. A bigger balance and a smaller basket can go together.

Expected inflation and realized inflation are different

For next year, you don’t yet know how much prices will rise. Write down the rate you’ve assumed. When looking back, use inflation measured over the period in question. Even then, the things you buy may not have changed in price at quite the same rate as the basket in the general index.

Check an appealing conclusion against the contributions

Try checking the source’s own example. It says the person who deposits once early on finishes ahead of someone who starts later but deposits repeatedly. Before comparing the chart’s bars, look at how much money the second person actually put in. At the source’s constant 8%, with no tax or fees, the later saver finishes with more even when each deposit goes in at year-end.

In this example the single deposit ends at about 217,245 units. The annual deposits end at about 731,059. The second person also contributed 25 times as much money in total. To study what starting earlier changes, first decide whether you mean equal amounts per deposit or equal total contributions. Those comparisons answer different questions.

Checking the amounts in a spreadsheet

The single deposit grows for the whole period. On the other path, each of 25 deposits stays in the account for a different length of time. With end-of-year payments, the last has no time to grow and the first grows for 24 periods. Add up their ending values. Moving every payment to the start of its year gives each one an extra period, multiplying the previous total by 1.08.

Questions to carry into the next percentage claim

Return to the opening 100 units. If the balance grew, how much did you add yourself? If you paid a fee, when did that money stop earning a return? If a payment is due later, where did you account for the wait? Finally, how much of the original basket could you buy with today’s balance?

Next time a large ending balance catches your eye, try changing one assumption in these calculators. Keep the same starting money and change only the fee, for instance. If that changes the answer substantially, you know which contract term or payment date deserves a closer look before you use the number.

The same questions come up at work. An outstanding invoice can’t cover today’s bills until the money arrives. A small charge that recurs each month can be worth a closer look too. For that problem, see the friction we have learned to live with. For the question of how work creates value over time, read building wealth beyond luck.

Beside a figure you intend to use, write the starting amount, time period, later deposits and fees. If you’re comparing purchasing power, add the date of the prices you used.
