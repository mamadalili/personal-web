Learn More Than One Skill by Doing

Yesterday you saved a lesson on writing headlines. Today you have a product page in front of you. What can you actually do with the lesson? Write a line, let someone read it and use the part they didn’t understand to shape the next try.

IN THIS EXPERIENCE

  • A saved lesson is not evidence of practice; skill becomes visible when you respond to a problem in a live setting.
  • Choose one skill and one small intention for each attempt, do the work, and keep one observation for the next round.
  • A person or an AI tool can direct your attention to an error or possibility; the main judgment and attempt stay with you.

A saved list is not a practice record

You save a video and bookmark a few articles alongside it. The explanations make sense, yet when you have to write the copy or ask a customer a question yourself, getting started feels surprisingly hard. Sam Elsner calls someone who keeps gathering material a “collector.” What’s missing may simply be a chance to try the work.

You watched a lesson on headlines and the explanation made sense. Now take a sample product page and write one. Show it to someone and ask, “Who do you think this is for?” A detail that seemed obvious to you may be invisible to them. When you revisit the lesson, you now have a particular question to take back to it.

Now reopen the lesson and look for what it says about the trouble your reader had. You know what you’re looking for this time. One line from the whole video might help with the headline. If you don’t yet have a reader, write a small version for the sample page so you’ll have something to show.

What was the last lesson you saved about? Pick a small part you could use today and see what comes out of it.

Multiple skills begin with one specific attempt

Elsner’s title leaves room to learn several skills over time. His suggestion for today is much smaller. Pick one you can actually try. Your interest in writing, design and analysis can wait its turn. Change all three on this page at once and it gets harder to see which choice helped the reader.

Name the skill by the work you’ll do. “Learn marketing” leaves you wondering what to write when the page opens. “Write a headline that tells buyers what this model is for” gives you something to try. The same goes for analysis. Did orders fall because fewer people visited, or because fewer of them finished checkout?

For this attempt the output is just the headline. If you also change the photograph, price and description, it’ll be hard to tell which clue helped the reader recognise the intended buyer. You can work on design or analysis another day. Today you’re practising how to identify the buyer in words.

Where does the skill actually come to life?

Woods and colleagues describe a taskscape in sport. A player has more than a movement to learn. The opponent, their teammates and what is happening on the field help determine when to use it. The paper is about sport. We can borrow a question for writing practice: what would show a writer, in a real situation, how their words were understood? Read the paper.

You could write ten elegant headlines in a notebook and still have no idea how a reader understands any of them. Open “Writing” to see how to bring a reader into the exercise. For another skill, look for the equivalent situation where a number, a decision or a customer’s answer shows you what you missed.

Choose a skill. These are instructional examples you can adapt to your own work.

Comfortable drills have a place, but they are not enough

Elsner draws on his own coaching experience. A player can execute a movement in a drill and still need to learn when to use it against an opponent. Copying a well-written headline can likewise help you get a feel for its structure. When you write one of your own, the meaning a reader takes from it adds a new part to the exercise.

Read the three tabs with your headline in mind. A worked example lets you see what the writer did. Rewriting it gives you practice with the move. Handing your own line to a reader gives you an interpretation you didn’t choose. If the client work is sensitive, start with a safe rehearsal.

Study

See the model

A good example shows where the writer placed an important word and how they kept the line short. After looking, write a line for your own model. You’ll see which choice you still need to make for yourself.

Safe rehearsal

Repeat the move

On a sample page you can change several headlines without disrupting a purchase. Try out the sentence length and where the model name goes. You still have to find out how a buyer will read it.

Live work

Respond to the setting

Show a version you’re allowed to share to one reader. They might be short of time, unfamiliar with the models or puzzled by something you didn’t expect. Write down what actually happened and limit your conclusion to what you saw.

If a mistake would have consequences, get familiar with the work on a sample first. When you’re ready and have permission, try a small part under real conditions so you can observe the feedback too.

Write one clear intention for the next attempt

Elsner suggests writing one sentence about what you intend to practise, then trying it for around ten minutes. The ten minutes are a way to get started. You might revise a paragraph in that time, while sensitive analysis or client work could take longer and need supervision. The part worth keeping in both cases is the opening question: what do you want to notice this time?

For sales, you might write, “Next time I’ll ask how the customer deals with the problem today before I suggest anything.” For writing, you could give the opening paragraph to someone new to the topic and ask what problem they think it describes. In both cases you know what you’ll do and what to listen for.

If the reader doesn’t understand the headline, maybe you tried to fit too much into one line. Perhaps they aren’t someone who would use the product. Write those down as possibilities and check one next time. A single response tells you very little about your overall ability to write.

Record what the lesson could not show you

Your reader might say, “I can tell what it is, but I can’t tell how it differs from the other one.” Write that down in one line. Next week, with only the draft in front of you, you may not remember whether they struggled to identify the product or to tell the models apart. A short note lets you revisit the right problem.

“I wasn’t very good today” gives you little to pick up tomorrow. Try “When the buyer asked whether support was included, I couldn’t give a clear answer because I hadn’t checked what the service covered.” Now you know what to find out before the next call. For a chart, name the unclear definition or missing record that made the conclusion unreliable.

For a practice note, the event is usually enough. You can leave out the buyer’s name and the private message. If a customer or colleague’s details really matter, check what you have permission to record and who can access it. If the case can’t be recorded safely, write down just the part you personally need to practise.

What could a short note look like?

For this example you could write, “I wanted readers to recognise who this model is for. My reader still had to ask. I’ll try putting the main use before the list of benefits.” It’s a fictional note that shows how an observation can shape the next attempt.

Try again with one deliberate change

Your note says the models were hard to tell apart. Put one verified difference in the next headline and ask the same question again. Leave the picture and price in place for now. If the reader sees the difference this time, you know which change deserves a closer look.

A new ad campaign might bring different visitors during the very week you change the headline. Record that beside the edit. You can see whether a reader understood the wording this time, but the change in wording alone can’t explain every rise or fall in purchases.

Elsner compares repeated attempts to earning experience points in a game. For a closer look at your own progress, put the two headlines side by side and explain the choice you made in the second. If several readers still stumble on the same word, get more specific feedback or practise that part on a simpler example.

A guide directs attention; they do not make the attempt

In the sports paper, one thing a coach can do is direct an athlete’s attention to a missed signal. A colleague can do something similar with your headline by asking, “Which word is meant to tell me this model is for me?” You still have to choose the word, but you know what to look for on the next attempt.

An AI tool can read your draft and point to an unclear line or a question it leaves open. Check its suggestions against the actual product and a reader’s response. If today’s aim is to practise choosing words, write the headline yourself first. If delivery speed matters more, you may choose to hand over more of the work, knowing which decision you’ll get less practice making.

A preprint about writing essays with ChatGPT reported differences in electrical brain activity measured by EEG and in recall of the text. It involved a particular task and a limited sample, and there is a published critique of its methods. It doesn’t establish that AI assistance in general damages the brain or harms learning. For your headline, ask a smaller question. Can you explain the choice and make it yourself next time?

What if there’s no colleague or mentor available?

Compare your work with a reliable example you’re allowed to use. You could ask a reader one specific question or have an AI tool point out ambiguous wording. For work that will affect a high-stakes medical, legal or financial decision, have a qualified person review it.

Carry a skill forward through a real connection

A question you learnt to ask in a customer conversation might help you write a product page. That’s one possible transfer of a skill. Find out what the buyer asks before choosing, then open the page with the answer. A phrase that works in conversation can be unclear on a page. Give it to a reader and find out which part of the skill carried over.

An analyst used to asking “What was divided by what to get this rate?” has a good question to bring to a marketing report. Writing an ad still takes knowledge of the audience and where they’ll see it. The question about the figure travelled across. The rest of the task still needs practice of its own.

Elsner calls this his “30% rule” and explains that he isn’t measuring anyone’s skill level. His point is that you can try something limited before you become an expert, such as writing one headline and seeing whether it makes sense. If your work affects a high-stakes decision, involve someone qualified to check that part.

How can I tell whether the skill helped with the next task?

Name what the two settings shared and what was different. In the second task, which choice did you change, and who could use the result? If you can’t point to an example yet, you still need a chance to test the connection.

Define one real attempt for this week

This week’s task might be that one headline. Find the page, see which version you’re allowed to edit and choose someone who could read it. If you can’t try it on the live site, use a sample. By the end of the week you can have something you didn’t have before: a reader’s account of what your sentence meant to them.

Keep one observation for next time. If a reader stopped to ask “Who is this for?”, try writing the next draft around that question. Later, see whether the same question helps in a sales conversation. For another exercise that starts with real work, read how to make your expertise understandable. Before you begin, use the checklist to decide what you’ll look for this time.

Sources & methodology
