A Day to Rethink Your Direction

You keep meaning to show someone your project, yet here you are changing the page colours again. Spend a day noticing what happens at that moment and you may find one small thing to try differently tomorrow.

What can one day actually do?

A fresh notebook starts with “This week I’ll finally show someone my project.” A few days later nobody has seen it. Dan Koe suggests taking a close look at an ordinary day. In the morning, think about where you’re heading. During the day, notice what actually happens. In the evening, use those observations to decide what you’ll try next.

Give yourself a day to notice something that usually slips by. Say you meant to send someone a proposal on Tuesday. That evening the project page has new colours, and your message is still in drafts. Before making yourself the same promise for Wednesday, think back to when you opened the colour settings. What made it hard to send the message then?

For today, catching the moment you switched to the colours is enough. It gives you something specific to look for tomorrow when you ask whether the message moved any closer to being sent.

There is space between “I want to” and what I do

So far you know the page has new colours and nobody has seen the offer. Maybe it was hard to say what the customer would receive. Maybe time was short, or you were worried about the reply. A clearer offer might help with the first difficulty. A short, free stretch of time could address the second. The changed page alone can’t tell you which it was.

Koe asks what the thing you did made easier. While you were working on the colours, for example, you didn’t have to hear anyone’s opinion of the offer. That might explain Tuesday. You might also have been tired, caring for someone or waiting for permission to send it. Look at what happened that day and leave the explanation open.

A research review found that stronger intentions did not produce equally large changes in behaviour. “I’ll take it seriously tomorrow” still leaves you deciding what to do when the file opens. Write something you’ll be able to recognise. When will you open it, what will you do and what might get in the way? Research review.

Three situations, three different obstacles

Open the project example. You can see that the message hasn’t been sent. Worry about criticism is one possible explanation. Another is that you can’t yet say what the customer would receive. Look for a way to tell those apart. The other two examples ask a similar question about learning and changing careers. All three are hypothetical.

Separate the pattern from the label

Choose a case, then ask the same three questions about your own work.

If two explanations still seem possible, look for a way to tell them apart. You might need one person to read the draft and tell you what they think the offer means. Or you might need time you currently don’t have. Each leads to a different question for tomorrow.

Divide the day into three short notes

Koe splits his exercise into morning, daytime and evening. We’ll use shorter questions and follow the same proposal. In the morning, write what you hope will have happened by tonight. During the day, note the moment you put the message aside. In the evening, read both notes and look for one thing you could change tomorrow. Each tab walks through one part.

Morning

Write two concrete pictures

Write about Tuesday. Who did you want to see the offer, and what was still undone that evening? Now picture two ordinary days. What would your day look like if this kept happening? What would you actually do differently on a better day?

Daytime

Watch what actually happens

The next time you mean to send the proposal, notice what you open instead. Where are you and what are you missing? Write one line about what actually happened, even if you don’t know the reason yet.

Evening

Turn the notes into one choice

Put the morning note beside what you saw during the day. Which explanation could you test tomorrow? If sending the message requires permission, time or help, make space for that in the next action.

What if I don’t have a whole day?

Use a few short openings. Write about the project now, pay attention the next time you open the page and compare the notes later. If you missed part of what happened, leave it open until you have something to observe.

Attach tomorrow’s action to a situation

How would you recognise “trying harder” tomorrow? Research on if–then plans links a situation you can recognise to something you can do when it occurs. For the project, that might be “If I open the colour settings tomorrow morning, I’ll first send the offer to someone it concerns and ask what they understand from it.” Research overview.

Try reading “I’ll send it if I have time” as an instruction for tomorrow. How will you recognise that moment? “When the morning meeting ends, I’ll open the draft and ask one customer what they understand from it” gives you a cue you can recognise and an action you control. If you need permission to contact someone first, asking for it can be your first step.

Leave the sentence where you’ll see it tomorrow. Start with a moment you can recognise and follow it with an action you can take. Give yourself something to look at tomorrow evening, such as the message you actually sent.

Check the plan before closing the page

Read the sentence you wrote for tomorrow against the four checks below. If it says “after the meeting,” you can point to a starting cue. If it only says “make progress,” you can’t yet tell what you’d look for tomorrow evening. Revise the part you can’t point to.

Will I know what to do tomorrow?

Each check needs evidence. This is a follow-up list, not a score.

You might tick all four boxes today and still find the message in drafts tomorrow. Did the meeting take place? Was the task too large for the time you had? Were you missing something? The answer tells you which part to change on the next attempt. The article on recurring friction uses a similar approach to repeated problems at work.

The following days reveal the change

It takes more than a day to see what might become a habit. Research on habit formation found that repetition in everyday settings mattered and that the time it took a behaviour to become automatic varied across people and behaviours. Watch for the same cue next week. How often did it occur, and what did you do then? That tells you more about your own days than a universal deadline would. Habit formation study.

Come back to the message next week. Maybe you sent it and the reply showed that the offer needed work. Or maybe the meeting was cancelled and your cue never happened. Those two observations suggest different next steps. If you’re exploring independent work, the solo-business article follows an offer through to delivery.

Sources & methodology

Dan Koe’s piece is a personal essay and reflection proposal, not evidence that a life can be reliably changed in a day. The research here concerns the intention–action gap, if–then planning and repetition; it does not predict anyone’s personal outcome.
