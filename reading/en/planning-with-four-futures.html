One Plan, Four Futures

Before signing a long contract, try living with its terms in four different futures. Which clause would you change today, and which decision can wait?

IN THIS EXPERIENCE

  • Keep the contract the same in all four settings so you can see which clause starts to matter.
  • Before paying for a second system, try exporting an open order from the first one and reading it.
  • For a decision that can wait, name the event that would bring you back to the contract.

Begin with a present decision

A shop is about to sign a multiyear deal for its ordering software. The price works and the staff know the system. One question remains on signing day. If the terms change, how will the shop move its order history elsewhere? That’s a good use for a scenario, an imagined future with specific conditions in which you can try out a decision you face today.

What does the source provide?

BCG describes four different versions of 2050. Keep the same shop and contract in mind as you explore them. The settings come from the report. The questions the shop might ask a software supplier are exercises for this article. Holding the contract still makes it easier to see which assumptions matter.

Test the same decision in four settings

AI Abundance

AI Abundance

In this setting, productivity rises and countries adopt shared standards. Back in the shop, imagine that similar software becomes easy to build. What service would a customer still pay for? It might be help choosing the right product. Check whether the contract gives the team room to improve that service.

Battling Blocs

Battling Blocs

In this world, trade splits into blocs and some suppliers become hard to reach. What happens to orders in progress if the software company can no longer serve your region? Export a file and see whether another tool can read the address, item and status for each open order.

Climate Coalition

Climate Coalition

Here, countries cooperate after climate shocks. Imagine a wholesale customer asking for evidence of the resources used to produce an item. Can the shop keep that evidence with the product record? Pick one claim and see if a colleague can trace it from an order back to the relevant supplier document.

Digital Darwinism

Digital Darwinism

In this setting, large companies wield more power and inequality is high. If the platform changes its access terms, how would the shop know which buyer needs a call about a delay? Check the ways the shop is allowed to contact customers and the records it will retain when the contract ends.

Four futures, four different pressures

The four settings put different pressures on the same deal. In one, a tool might get cheaper to build. In another, the shop might lose access to it. Asking whether the future sounds good or bad tells you little about the business. Ask which promise in today’s contract would no longer be enough to keep taking orders.

“Export your data at any time” sounds reassuring. Yet if the export needs the same software to open it, having the file won’t help the shop switch. The manager now has a precise request to make before signing. Ask the supplier to show a sample order that can be read outside the system.

Why are these not probability forecasts?

The report draws on trends, history and interviews to construct its settings. A number within a scenario describes that imagined world. It doesn’t tell you the odds of that world coming about. For the contract exercise, you only need to understand what follows if a given assumption changes.

Run one decision through all four worlds

Pick a clause that will be hard to change after signing. Suppose the shop can export records only until the contract ends. In which setting is that enough time? In which might the supplier also be unavailable that week? Explain what each answer means for one customer whose order is still in progress.

Separate what you can do this week from what can wait. Trying an export is a small job. Paying for a second system costs more and might serve no purpose yet. For that decision, choose a signal somebody can observe, such as written notice that access terms are changing. The buyer then knows exactly when to reopen the question.

If every scenario ends in “buy another system,” look again from the counter staff’s point of view. On the day a customer is waiting, what would they actually need to keep working?

Look for moves that work across several worlds

One broad idea in the report is to keep tools and working methods adaptable. The shop can turn that into a practical check. A second member of staff should be able to open the export, understand the columns and find an unfinished order. A backup earns its place when someone can use it.

Put a cost against that flexibility. Training a colleague, maintaining an export and trying a second supplier each take time. A cheap test that helps in several futures has a stronger case for doing now. An expensive purchase useful in only one might be worth postponing until its warning signal appears.

Turn the discussion into choices

Set the four answers beside each other. Perhaps several point to the same step, such as trying a data export before signing. That’s the idea behind a low-regret move. Even if those futures never arrive, the shop has spent little and learned something about the records it depends on today.

When should we reopen the plan?

Add a note to the contract file. “When the supplier sends notice of new terms, the buyer will test whether open orders can still be exported.” At the next review, look for that specific signal. The next buyer will know when to revisit the decision and what evidence to bring.
