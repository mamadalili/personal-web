Building Wealth Beyond Luck

You’ve helped a shopkeeper work out why their site gets so few enquiries. Which part of that work would get better the next time? Follow that question through eight ideas from Naval, starting with understanding a problem and choosing what’s worth repeating.

What does “without luck” mean?

Stijn Noorman writes about running a one-person business. He credits Naval Ravikant with changing how he thinks about wealth and picks out eight ideas that helped him. Reading his account raises two questions. What worked for Noorman himself? Which part could you try in your own circumstances?

Let’s follow a hypothetical job. A shopkeeper says, “People visit my site, but hardly anyone asks about buying.” You could follow the contact route, find where a visitor gets stuck and show the owner what happened. You can’t know whether the next shop will hire you. What you learn about finding that problem can carry into your next job. Timing, market conditions and access to money still shape the choices available to you.

Think of one job you do. If you pick it up again next month, what did you learn the first time that you won’t have to discover all over again?

Choose work that improves with repetition

Consider Naval’s idea of working with people over time. On the first job you might only just discover the difference between “hardly anyone calls” and “people call about things we don’t sell.” On the next job you know to ask that early. If you work with the same owner, they can also see whether you kept your last promise. That’s what has a chance to build over time.

Sticking with something isn’t always the right call. If several shopkeepers tell you they have a different problem, listen. But if nobody has seen your first attempt and you’re tempted away by a fashionable topic, you haven’t yet found out what was useful about the work.

How do I recognize a long-term collaborator?

Noorman draws on Naval’s three traits: intelligence, energy to do the work and integrity. They are not labels to assign in one meeting. Look at delivered work, kept commitments and behavior when things get difficult. For an important partnership, a small shared project can reveal more than hours of talk.

Name a job you could start with a better question next time. If all you have is “help a business grow,” narrow it to something like “find out why customers call instead of submitting the form.” The solo-business article follows a question like that into a specific offer and delivery.

Build knowledge fitted to a particular problem

On the shop job you might be able to interview customers, read the page as a buyer and spot a broken form. That particular combination, put to work on a real problem, is one way to understand specific knowledge. Plenty of people can do each part. What you can test is whether putting them together helps this owner find out why so few people call.

Noorman suggests noticing work you return to willingly even when it looks difficult to someone else. You might enjoy finding out why a customer abandoned a form. Does the shop owner care? Ask when it last happened and what they did about it. That begins to tell you whether your curiosity meets a need.

On one side of a sheet, list things you learn without being pushed. On the other, write down questions people have actually brought you. If something appears on both sides, make a small version for one person. If they don’t use it, find out whether it missed the problem or whether they couldn’t see how to use it.

Does “specific knowledge cannot be taught” mean learning is useless?

No. Foundational skills can be taught. The useful point in Naval’s phrase is that standardized training alone does not create a personal edge. Experience, choice of problem and combination of skills matter. It is not a reason to dismiss education or learnable craft.

Choose leverage for the work you actually do

Write a guide to testing a contact form once and several shop owners could use it on their own sites. That’s an example of leverage. More people can use the work without requiring the full original effort each time. The source names four ways to do this through people, capital, code and media. You may be able to make a small tool or guide yourself. Whether anyone needs it and can find it are separate questions.

Start with code. If you keep opening the same forms by hand to see whether they work, a small tool might repeat that check. Now open media. This time it’s the explanation for shopkeepers that can travel. As you explore the four options, ask which part of the work would actually get used again.

What are you trying to make repeatable?

Open each lever, then replace the example with one from your own work.

Before writing the guide for lots of shops, hand it to one owner and see if they can use it to test their form. If they’re stuck on the first step, that explanation may need work. The solo-business article also explores which parts you might do yourself and which you could give to an AI tool.

Judgment decides what leverage multiplies

Noorman asks a question before choosing any of those methods. How do you know the thing you plan to do more of is helping? If a shop owner says the site needs more visitors, first see how far someone who visits today can get. That might show you where the original assumption needs work.

The shop owner could buy more advertising tomorrow. If the contact form fails on a phone, more people will just reach the same broken form. Use the two tabs to see which question spending money would answer and which one an inspection might settle. If the form works, you can ask whether the page is reaching the right buyers.

Scale first

Expand the current route

If real customers have already validated the offer and buying path, and reaching relevant people is the constraint, more distribution can make sense. Needed evidence: which traffic brings qualified enquiries and whether its cost is bearable. Without that evidence, more visits only enlarge the top of the funnel.

Investigate first

Locate the main unknown

A few relevant conversations, reading the page from a buyer’s perspective and testing the contact path may reveal the friction. This does not guarantee a faster or better outcome; it makes the assumption clearer before costs rise. If distribution is the constraint, scale it then.

Our shop is fictional. In a real business, look at the cost of bringing in an enquiry, whether it’s relevant and whether the team has time to respond. If orders are coming in faster than people can handle them, the article on team capacity explores that next decision.

Stop competing by imitation

One person might start by inspecting the shop’s code. Someone who has worked a counter might first ask, “What do people ask you in the shop before buying?” and then look for that answer on the page. Your experience becomes visible when you can show what that question helped uncover. That’s a concrete difference worth describing to a prospective customer.

Spotting a failed form might feel easy because you’ve dealt with one many times before. The owner can’t see that history, but they can repeat the steps on their own phone. Show them where the button fails and what you checked. If you later discover your diagnosis was wrong, be ready to correct it. Naval includes that responsibility alongside doing work that reflects your own experience.

Practice the work behind “business”

Selling the website review involves several different skills. You need to find the problem, reach a shopkeeper who has it and explain what they’ll get at the end. Noorman includes writing alongside building and selling because a short message may be someone’s first encounter with your offer. If they can’t make sense of it, you may never get to show them the work.

Let the next difficulty point you towards what to learn. If customers keep asking what they’ll receive, rewrite the offer. If price or the contract gives you trouble, that’s a good reason to study it. If every order starts from scratch, write down the steps that repeat. If nobody opens the report, ask why before buying more advertising.

Try making one sample report. Can you do the check it promises? When an owner reads it, can they tell how it might help? Do they actually have that problem? Take whichever question you can’t answer into your next attempt.

More income is not the same as more freedom

Say more shops hire you and your income goes up. If you use those bookings to take on a new monthly expense, the bill will still arrive in a quiet month. You might then have to take a job that leaves little room for learning or better work. That’s the kind of lasting commitment Noorman has in mind when he discusses lifestyle creep.

Look at your own commitments. If no new work comes in next month, which bills will still be due? Which commitments could you change? How much time or money would that leave for choosing the next job? Someone supporting a family will have a different answer from someone in different circumstances.

What if essential costs have risen?

Not every increase is lifestyle inflation. Rent, health care, family care and changing prices may be beyond your control. Do not reduce those pressures to a personal discipline failure. Separate what is essential, what can be renegotiated and what requires steadier income or support.

Read “productize yourself” carefully

When Naval talks about turning what you do into a product, think of the owner trying to understand what they’re paying for. A part of your work can become a service with a clear starting point, a defined deliverable and responsibilities you’re willing to take on. Explain the price or how you set it so the customer can decide.

For example, an owner could supply one page link, you could try the enquiry process on a phone and return a note showing where it gets stuck. Say upfront whether the job also includes fixing the site. After doing this a few times, you may see parts of the report you can prepare from a template. If nobody has asked for the service yet, a polished sales page won’t tell you whether there’s demand.

After delivering a sample, look at both sides. Could the owner use it to make a decision about the site? Could you deliver the same quality next time with a better idea of the time involved? If you’d like to start by changing one work habit, the one-day reflection helps you choose a first step.

Leave with one decision

Before building a tool or hiring someone to help with site reviews, try answering a check below from your own experience. Has a shopkeeper seen a finished review and told you which part helped? If nobody has seen one yet, you have a next step. The checklist can’t calculate future earnings.

What do I know before scaling?

This is a decision list, not a score for ability or wealth.

For the shopkeeper in our example, a first version could be small. Try the contact route on a phone, show the owner what happened and ask what decision they could make with that information. Their answer gives you something to go on when deciding whether to repeat or change the service.

Allow about 10–13 minutes to read the article. Set aside some extra time if you want to work through the checklist using a real job.
