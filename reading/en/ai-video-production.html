AI Video: From Idea to Final Cut

A gift box should open in your video, but it changes size and loses its ribbon. Let’s use that example to work through the story, frames and movement before generating a clip, then move on to editing.

Before generating, decide what we should see

You’re making a video for a gift shop. You have an image of a teal box and ask the model to open its lid slowly. The lid opens, but the box grows and its red ribbon disappears halfway through. You try again and get a different problem. Before another attempt, it helps to ask which part of the shot was still unclear.

“Make an engaging video of the box” is a reasonable conversation starter, but it doesn’t describe the event yet. For our example: the base stays on the table, the lid rises gently, and the ribbon attached to it moves along with it. The ending holds on the box. Now we know what to look for. This is an example developed for the article, not a real campaign result.

In the source article, Eian describes a workflow using Claude Code, Codex and Seedance. The idea we’ll use here is that some problems can be found before generating the video. If the story or intended movement is still unclear, changing the model alone won’t resolve that uncertainty. We’ll follow the same box through the process and make each decision explicit.

Which part should each tool handle?

In Eian’s setup, Claude Code brings together the story, production plan and instructions. Codex with an image tool helps prepare the stills, Seedance generates the clips, and ffmpeg and Python handle technical editing tasks. Those connections were configured for his project. Installing the applications doesn’t mean they can automatically hand work to each other.

You don’t need the same tool combination to understand the process. Separate the outputs: a story and shot list, suitable still images, then video clips. A text assistant can help organise the idea and question its weak points. Making the images or video requires actual access to those capabilities. If the tools aren’t connected, you can hand the files between them yourself.

Some decisions remain yours: who the video is for, what it should show, what the product actually looks like and how much you’re willing to spend. If the shop’s box has a thin lid and a particular ribbon, a beautiful but different box won’t do. Before submitting a paid shot, inspect the images and know which files are being sent and how the charge is calculated.

Turn the idea into shots we can actually plan

We now know what the box should do. Next, decide where the viewer sees it from. A shot is a continuous piece of footage between cuts. We could establish the box on a table, move to a closer view of it opening, then hold on the product. A few sketches of that sequence make a storyboard. They don’t need to look polished yet; they need to make the sequence visible enough to discuss.

Choose one main event for each shot. If the camera circles the box while the lid opens, several people react and text appears, a bad result becomes harder to diagnose. That shot isn’t forbidden; it simply gives us more things to control at once. For a very short video, the close view of the lid opening might already communicate the message.

Establish

Let the viewer understand the object

The closed box on a table, framed clearly enough to establish size and colour. Keep the camera fixed. If the product will move, leave room for its destination.

Main event

Make one important change readable

The lid rises slowly while the base stays in place. Specify that the ribbon is attached to the lid and travels with it. A closer view can make this change easier to read.

Ending

Leave time to understand the result

Hold on the product. Add an exact name or offer during editing rather than relying on generated lettering. Reserve space for it when planning the image.

If we move the camera, we should know what that movement reveals. Moving closer could show ribbon detail; moving along the product might reveal its surface. But when the main event is the lid rising, a fixed camera may be enough. We don’t need everything moving at once just to make the image feel alive.

What stays fixed, and what should move?

Before planning movement, we need a reference image whose appearance we accept: colour, proportions, lid shape, ribbon placement and table surface. Use that reference for the next image and say what should change. If we keep starting over with “a teal box”, the model may make a similar but different product each time. Similarity isn’t always enough when you’re presenting a product.

Keeping those details coherent is visual continuity. That doesn’t mean everything must be identical in both frames. The lid should move, but the base shouldn’t grow for no reason. If the camera deliberately moves closer, the box looking larger makes sense; with a fixed camera, that change needs investigation. Decide the shot’s intention first, then judge which changes belong.

Look at both frames at full size, then shrink them to something closer to a phone display. Details that work on a monitor may barely register on a phone. Match the intended dimensions and aspect ratio before submission too. A different frame shape adds another mismatch that we can resolve now rather than leave for the model.

Let’s find the mismatch between two frames

For the exercise, imagine another shot of the same box. This time the lid stays closed and the whole box moves toward screen-right, with the camera fixed. We’ve deliberately added a few problems below. Each checkbox corrects that detail in the simple drawings. Work through the box’s presence, size, ribbon and direction, and see why the ending still differs from the plan.

When the four details line up, we’ve resolved those particular mismatches. We still don’t know how smoothly the frames will connect, whether the box will deform along the way or what the audio will sound like. The tool doesn’t analyse a real image or clip. It helps us practise what to compare before generation; judging the actual footage comes afterwards.

How do we describe the movement to the model?

We have the frames, but something still needs to happen between them. “Make the box move attractively” leaves the model to guess our intention. Say what moves, where it starts and ends, and what the camera does. Describe direction from the viewer’s perspective: screen-right, not a character’s right hand. In the opening shot, the lid rises while the base stays on the table. The wording should make that distinction clear.

The source breaks the instruction into separate parts. For our example, we can cover appearance, framing, the product, the event, movement sequence, audio and constraints. This makes undecided details easier to spot. We don’t need to pad the instruction with unrelated detail; we need to remove uncertainty about this shot. If one line asks for a fixed camera and another asks it to orbit the product, resolve that contradiction before submitting.

A sample instruction for lifting the lid

Appearance: match the reference, with a teal box and a red ribbon attached to the lid. Framing: close view, fixed camera. Object: the box base stays on the table. Event: the closed lid rises gently, carrying its ribbon. Sequence: begin closed, lift smoothly, then hold briefly. Audio: make this version silent for later editing. Constraints: preserve box shape, size and lighting; generate no lettering. This is sample wording, not a tested prompt with guaranteed results.

Providing first and last frames is one way to guide the movement. Seedance’s official guide describes that input mode, but exact capabilities can vary between versions. For real production, check the documentation for the version you’ll use. A model name or price mentioned in a post may not apply to your next project, so check the current details before spending.

Try the difficult part before making every shot

Most of the planned shots might be straightforward, with one difficult part: the lid needs to rise while carrying its ribbon correctly. Try that part early. Making the other shots won’t resolve this movement if it keeps failing. The test can tell us to simplify the action, revise the reference image or show the event differently through editing.

Set a spending limit and an attempt limit before starting the shot. Still images can use quota or money too; video isn’t necessarily the only cost. Choosing a lower resolution also depends on the job. If product detail matters, check that the test preserves it. Enlarging the image afterwards won’t turn an incorrect box shape or colour into the right product.

Keep each version under a separate name so you can compare them. A new attempt might improve movement while damaging the box shape. Deleting the earlier one makes that comparison harder. Add a brief note alongside the file: what changed this time, what improved and what remains wrong? That gives the next attempt a clear starting point instead of another round of guessing.

Find the problem before deciding to generate again

The clip is ready, but something looks wrong. First identify the defect. A few damaged ending frames might be removable with a trim. Incorrect lettering can be replaced with exact text during editing. But if the box itself changes shape mid-movement, joining shots won’t necessarily fix it; that section needs repair or regeneration. The remedy depends on what actually went wrong.

Start editing with the order of shots, the cuts and the final hold. Then watch and listen to movement and sound together. The picture may look fine while a sound effect misses the visible action, or unwanted music remains in the output. A few still images can’t reveal those problems. If an assistant has only inspected extracted frames, the audio and fluidity of the movement haven’t been fully reviewed.

At the end, watch without adding an explanation, or show the clip to someone who doesn’t know the idea. Can they follow what happened? Does the product look right? Is the text readable on a phone? Because we know the production process, we may fill gaps in our heads. A fresh viewer can help us notice where the video still leaves something unclear.

What should we keep for the next video?

When the piece is finished, keep a sheet with its message, reference image, intended movement and fixed details. Add the defects you saw and the effects of changes you actually reviewed. That gives the next piece a useful starting point. Record the experience of that test: an instruction working once doesn’t mean it will produce the same result for every product and model.
