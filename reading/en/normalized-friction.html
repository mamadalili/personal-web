The Cost of Friction We Accept

The driver schedule is ready. Then the calls to rearrange shifts begin. Follow the work those calls are doing before you decide whether to get rid of them.

Look past “that always happens”

The driver schedule is ready, but nobody sends it yet. First someone makes a few calls to find out whether the drivers can actually take their shifts. In this example the calls have become so routine that anyone asked how long scheduling takes might leave them out. A tool that produces the file faster could leave every call in place.

The phone calls in this example are fictional. The figures below come from D-Wave’s account of work with a customer. You can ask the same question about your schedule, but the answer depends on your team’s own time and quality records.

Follow one case through

Pick a shift that changed and trace it. Where did the driver’s request arrive, who saw it, and when did the change reach the schedule? Keep time spent doing the work separate from time spent waiting for someone to respond. ASQ’s guide describes how to observe the movement of information, the work itself and the waiting in between. ASQ guide.

Perhaps the driver gave plenty of notice, but their message never reached the scheduler. The message time and the version of the schedule can help you check. “People are slow to reply” leaves both people guessing about what to change. Once you know which piece of information went missing, you can try a specific way of getting it through.

Read the scheduling case with its label

There’s a real scheduling case with a named source. D-Wave sponsored a Retail Dive article about its work with Pattison Food Group. In D-Wave’s account, three to four schedulers drew up weekly driver plans province by province for more than 100 stores. They spent about 80 person-hours a week on it. The vendor says its hybrid scheduler brought that down to 15 while maintaining a threshold of 95% of demand fulfilled.

Person-hours add up the time each person spent. The bars let you compare the amount of scheduling work the vendor reports before and after. They don’t tell you the cost of the tool or the quality of every schedule. The 95% figure describes a threshold the vendor says the retailer maintained. You can’t use the two bars to work out a different store’s financial return.

Would another shop save the same amount of time?

Its store count, shift rules and data may be quite different. Find out what it takes to produce a usable schedule today, then include the cost of connecting the tool and dealing with daily exceptions.

Trace the problem to its real constraint

Your own shop may have a perfectly good shift schedule. Perhaps the warehouse keeps adding spare stock or support spends part of every day chasing correct addresses. Open the case that looks most familiar. The question inside helps you find out which problem the extra work is trying to prevent.

Which workaround is visible?

Choose a case to find the first question you could ask the person doing the work.

These are examples for examining your own work. If the driver gave notice in time and the message got lost, recording it where the scheduler will see it may be enough to try first. If the information is already current and finding a workable combination of shifts is still hard, you have a specific reason to test a scheduling tool.

Match the repair to the cause

Missing inputs

Clarify acceptance

If requests keep coming back incomplete, ask the receiving person for one they can use. Request the necessary information up front, then check whether the next case goes through or simply takes longer to fill in.

Waiting for a decision

Make authority explicit

When several people are waiting for approval, write down the decision one request needs and who may make it. With the next request, find out whether work moves or still waits for the same reason.

Rekeying

Find the authoritative record

If someone types the order twice, decide which record to trust when the copies disagree and where a correction belongs. Match the two versions of one order by hand before asking an automated transfer to carry the same information.

Inspect the whole route

When the next schedule is ready, start counting at the same point you did before. Ask how often drivers had to call after receiving it and whether customer orders still had drivers to cover them. If calls to the scheduler have fallen but support now fields the same calls, you know exactly where the work went.

When is the call worth keeping?

If that final call is the only way to catch a last-minute shift change or an uncovered route, find out what would catch it instead. You may be able to reduce routine calls and leave a way to call when a genuine exception comes up.
