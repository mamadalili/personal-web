How to Think Like a Chess Player

Four empty seats for the Friday class

Friday’s printmaking class has twelve stools. Eight people have paid. Would you buy an ad, cut the price or write to the people who asked about the last class? The choice turns on something you haven’t learned about them yet.

IN THIS EXPERIENCE

  • Four paid bookings by Friday is a small enough goal to guide a decision today.
  • People who almost booked can tell you something an impression count can’t.
  • Decide in advance what answer would make you change your plan.

Before you move, picture Friday

In chess you sometimes spot a pawn you could take for free. Your hand goes to the knight, then you stop. Once it moves, what’s left guarding your king? A discount code can be just as tempting. It takes ten minutes to make one. Finding out whether the price put anyone off is a different job.

Put yourself in a printmaker’s apron. It’s Wednesday. You’ve bought ink and paper for twelve. When you unlock the studio on Friday, you’d like twelve people tying on aprons. “Get more exposure” gives you little to go on today. Four more paid bookings by Thursday night gives you something you can work backwards from.

Choose one booking per four invitations and look at the four-seat column. Sixteen invitations fit inside your list of twenty. Now try one per eight. You’d need thirty-two people, twelve more than you have. This arithmetic won’t tell you how many will buy. It tells you which assumption makes your plan possible.

Read the pieces already on your board

Open the studio notebook. There are eight receipts and twenty people who asked to hear when the next class opened. Those are things you know. Whether those twenty have Friday morning free is a guess. Someone who asked about the next class last month may have been looking for an evening session. A name on the list isn’t money in the till.

There’s another constraint: between cleaning the press and taking orders to the post office, you have one free hour. Spend it filming an ad and nobody answers the waiting list today. That lost conversation is a cost, even if filming the ad is free.

Which of these three moves fits?

You could do any of these. Open each option and consider what would have to be true about your customers for it to earn that hour.

Invite the list

These people asked to hear from you

Send the date, time, price and booking link. Some may book. Others may explain what’s stopping them. For this Friday, start here.

Cut the price

Who actually said it costs too much?

If several people told you the fee is beyond their budget, price deserves a look. A blanket discount cuts the margin on each seat. It does nothing for someone working on Friday.

Run an ad

Maybe the right people haven’t heard about it

If the list is small and reaching new people has filled previous classes, an ad is worth considering. Check both its cost and the time left before bookings close.

Send an invitation people can answer

Write to a few of the people who asked for updates. Give the Friday time, price and booking link. Ask one short question: if they’d still like to come but this session doesn’t work, what gets in the way? Try the payment link on your own phone first. In your notebook separate payments, replies without payment and silence. Silence doesn’t belong in a column marked “too expensive.”

What if the replies change your plan?

By noon six people have replied: “I work Friday mornings. Do you ever run this in the evening?” You have evidence about those six. You don’t yet know if they’d pay for an evening seat, or even if the teacher is free. What would you do next?

Eight people have paid for the morning. What would you do first?

Move the whole class to the evening Eight people made plans for the morning. Six questions about evenings don’t change that commitment.

Check the teacher’s availability and offer a separate evening date That tests interest against an actual date and honours the morning bookings. Count evening reservations separately: they don’t fill Friday morning’s seats.

Send all six a discount All six mentioned timing. First see whether any of them will book a date they can actually attend.

Close the notebook on Thursday night, even if only ten people are coming. Write down how many you invited, how many paid and how many specifically asked about evenings. The next time your hand goes to the discount button, you’ll have a position from a previous game worth looking at.

Sources & methodology

Noorman supplies the five moves: choose an outcome, read the position, weigh alternatives, act and adapt. The print studio and all the numbers in the exercise are original illustrative examples.
