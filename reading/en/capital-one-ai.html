AI at Capital One: From Data to Daily Work

A customer hangs up and AI writes the call summary. How can the next colleague tell whether to rely on it? Capital One’s experience shows how much work sits behind that apparently simple use of AI.

Start with actual work

Imagine calling a bank about a payment. The next colleague who handles the case needs to know what you asked and what answer you got. If an AI summary leaves something out, how would they spot it? That’s one way to read MIT Sloan Management Review’s interviews with leaders at Capital One, a bank with a long history of using data to make decisions. Summarising calls is one of the uses they describe.

Capital One’s AI leader argues that looking only at this quarter’s return can miss changes that take time. There are still things you can find out about a call summary today. Which mistake would cause trouble for the customer? Who would spend time checking it, and how much would it cost to put that check in place?

Capital One is a large bank with years of investment in data and technology and banking rules to meet. A smaller team can ask some of the same questions. It still has to find out what happens in its own work.

Where does AI actually enter the work?

Open “Call summary” first. Here someone can compare what the model wrote with what the customer said. Then open “Agent assistance.” This time the suggestion is for someone still on the phone. The same mistake can have different consequences depending on who relies on it and when they can catch it.

Inspect the work behind each use case

Open each route and ask what data and review it needs.

The executives also mention software development and work on fraud. They give too little detail about those outcomes to support a comparison. With the three uses above, you can at least trace where the answer comes from and who needs to check it before relying on it.

Read the interview numbers carefully

An executive reports an 85% fall in quality errors in audit and compliance. Another figure is about 5,000 hours of manual work saved across 30 auditors over several months. The time for one particular task reportedly fell from five hours to three minutes. The first figure counts errors, the second adds up a group’s time and the third describes a single task. Keep that detail attached if you quote one.

To understand time saved, you’d want to know how long the task used to take and how the new time was measured. The article doesn’t give a full baseline or independent measurement for each figure. When quoting one, say who reported it and which task it covers. If you try call summaries yourself, decide in advance what counts as an error and record the time spent reviewing them.

What was the time worth?

Find out what the auditor could do with the time freed up. Building the tool, training staff, supervising it and correcting errors also cost money. The interview doesn’t give enough detail to put all of that together and calculate a financial return.

Five hours to three minutes, for that task only

Useful data is work in its own right

Back to the payment question. If the agent finds two guides giving different answers, an accurate summary of the call won’t settle which guide applies. They need to know which one covers this payment and who can correct an error. This helps explain Capital One’s idea of a data product. A team maintains information for others to use, explains what it covers and names someone responsible for its quality. Compare the two tabs with that question in mind.

Raw material

Data exists, but is not dependable

Two folders might hold different answers to the payment question. The person on the phone can find both without knowing which one still applies. Give the same folders to a model and it may put the old answer first.

Data product

There is an owner and correction route

The team closest to the payment guidance explains where it applies and keeps it current. Shared quality and access rules let other teams use it. For a small team, a list of answers with a named person responsible for each might be enough to start.

The bank also discusses knowledge graphs, a way to record how things relate to each other. Here we have a customer, a payment enquiry and an approved answer. First establish which enquiry the answer applies to and who approved it. Then you can tell what recording that relationship would do for the person taking the call. Rollout is more than handing out a tool

The bank’s leaders describe training staff for each new use. For a call summary, a practice exercise might be to compare it with the conversation and spot a missing promise. What happens next? The agent needs a way to correct the record or tell someone who can. Open the four items below and consider what it would take to check that one call.

What transfers beyond the bank?

A small team can start with a question like the payment enquiry. Pick a recurring request, find its approved answer and ask the colleague who normally handles it to review a few summaries. Can the next person tell what was promised, or must they listen to the whole call again? Their answer can guide the next step in that trial.

What is a careful takeaway from the Capital One interview?

If the next colleague still has to chase several people to make sense of one call, follow that work in the article on recurring friction. If the system will also act on the summary, the article on AI agents looks at which actions need approval.
